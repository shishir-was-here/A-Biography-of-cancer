1. Farber discovers that the laboratory is not enough

Sidney Farber's early leukemia experiments had shown that a chemical could temporarily suppress one of the deadliest childhood cancers. But the remissions were short, and the disease almost always returned. Finding a lasting cure would require many more drugs, more trained researchers, bigger clinics, and sustained financial support.

Farber's laboratory was tiny. His relationships with some senior hospital physicians were strained, and he lacked the resources for the large research program he imagined. He realized that he could not fight leukemia only by working harder at a microscope. He needed to create a public movement capable of supporting scientific work.

Mukherjee introduces an important new theme: major medical advances may require not only experiments but also organization, fundraising, storytelling, and political energy.

2. The polio campaign offers a model

Farber remembered the terrifying polio epidemics that had affected children during his earlier years. Polio could cause paralysis, including paralysis of the muscles required for breathing. Some children relied on mechanical ventilators known as iron lungs. The disease was an especially visible public nightmare because it often struck young people.

President Franklin D. Roosevelt, who had experienced paralysis, became a powerful public figure associated with the fight against polio. He helped establish institutions dedicated to rehabilitation and promoted a national foundation focused on polio research and public awareness.

The March of Dimes campaign demonstrated how a disease-focused movement could involve people across the country. Rather than depending only on a few wealthy donors, it encouraged ordinary households to contribute small amounts. Radio personalities, performers, and celebrities helped amplify the message.

Farber saw the essential lesson: a scientific cause becomes politically powerful when the public feels connected to it. Perhaps the same strategy could be used for childhood cancer.

3. The Variety Club and the “goodness of show business”

In 1947, representatives of the Variety Club of New England, including organizer Bill Koster, visited Children's Hospital. The Variety Club was associated with entertainment and theater professionals who had developed an interest in children's charitable causes.

The club's identity had been shaped by a remarkable earlier story. In 1928, a mother in desperate circumstances left a baby at the Sheridan Square Film Theater in Philadelphia with a note asking the entertainment community to care for her. The club helped raise the child, who became known as Catherine Variety Sheridan.

The story gained national attention and encouraged the club to see children's welfare as a central philanthropic mission. By the 1940s, its members were looking for charitable projects that could combine practical help with public engagement.

Koster's visit to Farber would connect that philanthropic experience with the emerging cancer-treatment movement.

4. Farber asks for something much bigger than a microscope

When Koster visited other researchers, he heard modest requests for equipment, including a new microscope. Farber was different. He described an ambitious plan for a large research and treatment center devoted to childhood cancer.

Farber needed more than laboratory supplies. He wanted an organization capable of funding experiments, attracting doctors, caring for patients, and keeping the public interested over many years. Koster recognized that Farber's vision could be turned into a compelling charitable campaign.

In early 1948, Farber and Koster launched the Children's Cancer Research Fund. Initial fundraising events raised meaningful money, but not nearly enough for the scale of Farber's dream. The organizers needed a public message that people would recognize and remember.

5. Why they needed a human face for cancer

A disease described only through laboratory figures or frightening medical language might not attract public sympathy. Koster and Farber sought a child who could stand for the many young patients affected by cancer. The idea was influenced by the Variety Club's earlier publicity around Catherine Sheridan.

But this was difficult. Many children in Farber's wards were severely ill, exhausted, and harmed by the effects of cancer or treatment. The organizers eventually focused on one patient who was well enough to appear before the public.

That child was Einar Gustafson, from a farming family in New Sweden, Maine. He did not have leukemia; he was receiving treatment for a rare form of intestinal lymphoma. He loved baseball, was quiet and self-possessed, and had been sent to Boston because the disease was extremely serious.

His real name was not simple for a national campaign. Farber and Koster decided to call him Jimmy.

6. The birth of Jimmy, a symbol of childhood cancer

The name Jimmy was deliberately ordinary and memorable. Rather than presenting a distant laboratory problem, the campaign would introduce listeners to a boy who seemed like someone living next door: a child who loved baseball and wished to return to everyday life.

This was an effective strategy, but it also raises questions. Einar was a real child with an individual life and illness. Turning him into a public symbol helped attract support, yet it risked simplifying his identity and treating his suffering as an instrument of publicity.

Mukherjee lets both dimensions remain visible. Jimmy was a person, and Jimmy was also becoming a carefully designed public image.

7. The radio broadcast of May 22, 1948

On May 22, 1948, radio host Ralph Edwards connected his show Truth or Consequences to a Boston hospital room. Listeners heard him speak to Jimmy and discuss the boy's love of the Boston Braves, his favorite baseball team.

What Jimmy did not know was that members of the Braves were waiting nearby. During the broadcast, players entered the room to surprise him. They brought gifts and spoke with him. Jimmy's excitement turned what might have been a medical appeal into an emotional radio experience.

The broadcast was short—around eight minutes—but the arrangement made listeners feel close to a specific child. They heard a boy delighted by baseball rather than a doctor reciting mortality statistics.

8. The fundraising appeal and public response

At the end of the program, Edwards appealed for donations to support research into childhood cancer. He explained that Jimmy wanted a television set to watch baseball games and connected the gift to a much larger fundraising goal.

The public response was extraordinary. Letters, checks, dollar bills, and coins poured in. Some envelopes were addressed simply to Jimmy in Boston. Children contributed their savings; sports fans and local organizations organized collections. Donations appeared at movie theaters, baseball games, and community events.

By May 1948, the campaign had brought in more than $231,000, far exceeding an initial target of about $20,000. A television eventually arrived for Jimmy. The fundraising movement became widely known as the Jimmy Fund.

A child named Jimmy Made a large, abstract disease feel personal

9. What Farber learned about public advocacy

The money mattered, but Mukherjee argues that the political lesson mattered even more. A disease could become a public cause through recognizable stories, symbols, and campaigns. In other words, changing the trajectory of cancer research might require changing the way society talked about cancer.

This was especially significant because cancer had long been an illness whispered about privately. The Jimmy Fund brought it into American living rooms through a radio show. A condition associated with silence became something families could acknowledge and support.

Farber was therefore transforming himself. He was no longer only a pathologist or an experimental cancer physician. He was becoming an advocate and organizer, learning to mobilize society on behalf of research.

10. The deeper meaning of Chapter 12

Science needs a public foundation. Research depends on laboratories, money, institutions, and political commitment. Stories can turn statistics into human concerns. Jimmy's ordinary love of baseball made it easier for listeners to empathize with children facing cancer.

But publicity has ethical complexity. A patient used as a mascot is still a person deserving dignity and privacy. A campaign can create hope without implying that a cure has already been found. Farber learned to use the power of public emotion, but the disease itself remained difficult to treat.

Chapter takeaway: The Jimmy Fund helped move cancer from the private hospital ward into public consciousness. Farber discovered that building support for research was itself a form of medical action—different from treating a patient, but crucial to the future of treatment.
