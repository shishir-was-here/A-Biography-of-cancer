A new question: What makes this cancer different?

In the previous chapter, chemotherapy researchers tried to defeat cancer through increasingly powerful combinations of drugs. The approach sometimes produced miraculous cures, but it also caused severe toxicity and often failed against common solid tumors.

This chapter introduces another approach: study the unique biology of a cancer, then attack a specific weakness.

Instead of beginning with the question, Which chemical kills the largest number of cells?, scientists could ask, What does this particular cancer cell need in order to grow?

Some cancers, Mukherjee explains, retain a dependence on biological signals used by the normal cells from which they arose. If that dependence can be identified, a treatment might block the signal without indiscriminately poisoning every dividing cell.

The chapter's title comes from the ancient principle that understanding an adversary can be more important than attacking blindly. To fight cancer intelligently, scientists must know the enemy—its origin, behavior, and vulnerabilities.

Charles Huggins: An unlikely cancer researcher

The story begins with Charles Huggins, born in Nova Scotia in 1901. He attended Harvard Medical School and eventually joined the University of Chicago as a urological surgeon in 1927.

Huggins was not a famous cancer biologist. His formal training in urology was surprisingly limited, and he spent several weeks learning the specialty from textbooks before taking up his appointment.

His new clinic attracted few patients, so he established a laboratory and began studying the physiology of the prostate gland.

The prostate is a small gland in the male reproductive system, surrounding part of the urinary tract. It produces fluid that helps support sperm. Huggins wanted to know what controlled the gland's growth and secretion.

What began as a study of normal physiology would unexpectedly lead to an important approach to cancer treatment.

The relationship between testosterone and the prostate

Huggins developed techniques to collect small amounts of prostate fluid from laboratory dogs. He noticed that the gland's function depended strongly on hormones produced by the testicles, especially testosterone.

When testosterone supply was removed through surgical castration, the dogs' prostates became smaller and produced less fluid. When testosterone was supplied again, the prostate regained function.

This demonstrated a powerful biological relationship:

Testosterone helps maintain the normal growth and activity of prostate tissue.

We can imagine testosterone as a signal received by prostate cells. The signal tells the cells to sustain certain functions and growth. Remove the signal and the gland's activity changes dramatically.

For Huggins, this was an important physiological finding. But he had not yet reached the surprising part of the story.

What happens to prostate cancer without testosterone?

Some of Huggins's laboratory dogs developed prostate tumors. Initially, he considered them unwanted complications in his normal-prostate experiments.

Then a new question occurred to him: if normal prostate cells depend on testosterone, might malignant prostate cells still depend on it?

At the time, many scientists assumed that cancer cells had completely abandoned the rules of normal biology. Malignant cells were believed to behave independently, endlessly dividing without needing the signals that regulated healthy cells.

Huggins discovered that this view was not always correct. When he removed the source of testosterone in dogs with prostate tumors, some tumors shrank dramatically.

The malignant cells had not forgotten their origin. They still depended on a normal hormone-driven growth mechanism.

This was a major conceptual breakthrough. A cancer could be abnormal yet remain dependent on a perfectly normal substance made by the patient's own body.

Hormone deprivation becomes a treatment

Huggins extended this reasoning to people with aggressive prostate cancer. If removing testosterone could shrink prostate tumors, perhaps hormone manipulation could offer relief to patients with advanced disease.

Surgical removal of the testicles was one method. Another approach involved administering estrogen-like hormones to reduce the body's testosterone activity, a strategy the book calls chemical castration.

The results were remarkable in some patients. Advanced prostate tumors shrank, symptoms improved, and the disease could be controlled for months.

Yet this was not a permanent cure. Many patients eventually relapsed as the cancer became resistant to hormone deprivation.

The importance lay in the new principle. Doctors could restrain some cancers by interrupting a specific biological dependency, rather than giving only general cell-killing poisons.

Contemporary hormone treatments and their safety profiles differ from the historical interventions described in the chapter. The book is explaining how the underlying principle was discovered, not recommending those old regimens.

Could another cancer depend on another hormone?

Huggins's discoveries suggested a question about breast cancer. Normal breast tissue is influenced by hormones, including estrogen. Could certain breast cancers also depend on estrogen for growth?

Long before scientists understood this hormone connection, a Scottish surgeon named George Beatson had encountered an intriguing observation about ovaries and breast tissue.

In the 1890s, Beatson removed the ovaries of three women with advanced breast cancer. The ovaries were not cancerous, and the operation seemed biologically unrelated to a breast tumor.

Yet the tumors shrank significantly.

When others repeated the technique on more women, however, the results were inconsistent. Some patients benefited; others did not.

Why would removing the same organ help one woman's breast cancer while failing for another? The puzzle remained unsolved for decades because doctors did not yet understand the relevant hormone-signaling system.

Estrogen provides part of the answer

Scientists eventually established that the ovaries produce estrogen, a hormone important in the development and maintenance of breast tissue.

Now Beatson's strange experiment made more sense. Removing the ovaries reduced estrogen, and some breast tumors appeared to shrink as a result.

But this explained only half the mystery. If estrogen mattered, why did some breast cancers fail to respond?

The answer would require a deeper understanding of how hormones act on individual cells.

Hormones do not affect all cells equally. Their effects often depend on cellular proteins called receptors, which recognize a particular hormone and help transmit its signal.

The important question became whether every breast tumor possessed the same estrogen-related receptor system.

Elwood Jensen discovers the estrogen receptor

Working in Chicago during the 1960s, chemist Elwood Jensen investigated how estrogen interacts with cells.

By tracking a radioactively labeled form of estrogen, he identified the estrogen receptor in 1968.

He then tested breast cancer cells and discovered an important difference: some tumors expressed the receptor, while others did not.

Researchers could now distinguish between:

  • ER-positive breast cancer: Cancer cells contain detectable estrogen receptors and may depend on estrogen signaling.
  • ER-negative breast cancer: Cancer cells lack significant detectable estrogen receptors and generally do not respond to therapies aimed specifically at that receptor.

This solved much of Beatson's old riddle. Women whose tumors depended on estrogen were more likely to benefit from removing the hormone source. Women whose tumors did not depend on estrogen had less reason to benefit from that strategy.

The cancers looked similar in the same organ but behaved differently at the molecular level.

Why this discovery changed the idea of cancer

Until this point, breast cancer was often treated as one category determined by its location in the body.

Jensen showed that two breast cancers could have fundamentally different biological needs, even if they were diagnosed in the same organ.

This distinction matters because a drug can be effective only if it acts on something the target cells actually possess or depend upon.

Imagine a medicine designed to block a particular door. It may work beautifully in a building where that door controls access, but it will do nothing in a building without the door.

Likewise, blocking estrogen signaling cannot be expected to work equally in tumors that do and do not depend on that pathway.

This is the early foundation of biomarker-guided treatment: identify a biological feature of a patient's cancer and use that information to help choose a medicine.

The strange history of tamoxifen

Meanwhile, British chemists at Imperial Chemical Industries (ICI) were developing hormonal compounds for contraception.

In 1962, a team led by Arthur Walpole, with chemist Dora Richardson, filed a patent for a compound called ICI 46474, later named tamoxifen.

The compound had originally been investigated as a potential contraceptive. But experiments showed that in many tissues it acted against estrogen's effects instead of supporting them. For its intended purpose, the project looked disappointing.

In an era when researchers were fascinated by powerful cytotoxic drugs, an unsuccessful contraceptive did not seem particularly interesting as a cancer treatment.

Walpole, however, saw another possibility. If tamoxifen could interfere with estrogen signaling, perhaps it could slow an estrogen-dependent breast cancer.

The drug's apparent failure in one field became the source of its promise in another.

Mary Cole tests an unlikely medicine (1969)

Walpole's team collaborated with Mary Cole, an oncologist and radiotherapist at the Christie Hospital in Manchester. Her colleagues and patients knew her as Moya Cole.

Cole treated women with advanced metastatic breast cancer, many of whom had very limited options. She agreed to try the former contraceptive compound.

In the late summer of 1969, Cole began a trial involving 46 women. She hoped for modest improvements at best.

But 10 women experienced conspicuous responses. Tumors in the breast became smaller; some metastases shrank; swollen nodes softened and pain diminished.

Many of these improvements were temporary, and some women eventually relapsed. But the result showed that a medicine designed around a specific biological pathway could suppress advanced cancer.

This was qualitatively different from simply screening for substances that killed cells as violently as possible.

Craig Jordan connects the drug to the target

In 1973, biochemist V. Craig Jordan studied why some breast cancers responded to tamoxifen while others did not.

Using receptor measurements, Jordan showed that tumors expressing the estrogen receptor were much more likely to respond to tamoxifen, whereas receptor-negative tumors did not show the same benefit.

This completed a long chain of scientific reasoning:

  1. Beatson noticed that some breast cancers responded when the ovaries were removed.
  2. Scientists identified estrogen as a key ovarian hormone.
  3. Jensen showed that some breast cancers had estrogen receptors and some did not.
  4. Tamoxifen could interfere with estrogen signaling.
  5. Jordan linked the presence of the receptor to the tumor's response to the drug.

For the first time, the book presents a cancer-cell feature, an identifiable drug target, and a treatment response connected by a coherent molecular explanation.

Why tamoxifen was different from older chemotherapy

The difference was not that tamoxifen could cure every breast cancer; it could not. Nor was it entirely free of side effects. Its importance was the logic behind its use.

Empirical cytotoxic approach Biological targeting approach
Find chemicals that damage dividing cells Identify a growth dependency or molecular target
Test across multiple tumors Select tumors likely to depend on the target
Often harms many normal dividing cells Can be more selective, though never perfectly harmless
Drug choice may be based on broad cancer type Drug choice can depend on a tumor biomarker

The new approach demanded more knowledge before treatment began. Researchers had to understand not only where a cancer was located but what was sustaining it.

The deeper meaning: Know the enemy, and know ourselves

The chapter overturns the belief that cancer cells are entirely independent of normal physiology. Many cancers continue to exploit the same growth signals their healthy ancestors once used.

That dependence can become a weakness. The body itself may be feeding a tumor through normal hormones, and interfering with that relationship can sometimes slow cancer.

But the result also reveals why there cannot be one universally effective medicine: different tumors have different receptors, mutations, and dependencies. Even cancers of the same organ may require different treatments.

Chapter takeaway: The next revolution in cancer medicine begins not by attacking harder, but by asking precisely what a particular cancer needs in order to survive.
