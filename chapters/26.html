The alternative: know what makes a cancer vulnerable

The desire for less indiscriminate treatment pushes researchers toward a different idea. Instead of searching only for increasingly powerful cell-killing chemicals, they might investigate the distinctive biology of each cancer and find an attack that works because of that biology. Some tumors depend strongly on particular hormonal signals, making hormone pathways an early model of selective treatment.

Charles Huggins and prostate cancer

Charles Huggins discovers that lowering androgen activity can cause some prostate cancers to regress. The principle is revolutionary: a tumor's growth may depend on signals from normal human physiology. Depriving the cancer of those signals can sometimes slow it without using a general poison against every dividing cell.

Breast cancer and estrogen

Researchers investigate whether estrogen plays a comparable role in some breast cancers. The effects are uneven: certain tumors respond to hormonal manipulation while others do not. What seems inconsistent becomes understandable when doctors begin identifying the estrogen receptor, a protein present in some tumors but absent from others.

Tamoxifen meets its molecular target

Tamoxifen can interfere with estrogen signaling in receptor-positive breast-cancer cells. Tumors lacking the relevant receptor are less likely to respond. Here the therapeutic logic becomes precise: identify a target, test whether the tumor possesses it, and use a drug that interferes with the target's activity. This foreshadows the molecularly targeted cancer medicines developed later.

What the new logic teaches

Two patients with 'breast cancer' may have biologically different diseases. A useful treatment depends on what each tumor requires for growth. Matching therapy to the tumor may produce better outcomes and less unnecessary toxicity than treating every cancer as the same enemy.
