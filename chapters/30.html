A mysterious lung-cancer epidemic in Britain (1947)

In the winter of 1947, British health officials faced an alarming pattern: lung cancer had become far more common than it had been just a few decades earlier. The book describes an increase of nearly fifteenfold over roughly twenty years in the recorded incidence of lung cancer.

Officials convened experts to investigate. The proposed explanations were scattered: polluted city air, coal smoke, road tar, automobile exhaust, influenza, industrial chemicals, even changes in sunshine. These possibilities were not absurd—many could affect health—but the meeting had no clear method for distinguishing the relevant exposure from the surrounding noise.

Something was causing many more people to develop lung cancer. What was it?

Why smoking was surprisingly easy to overlook

Cigarettes were ordinary objects in mid-twentieth-century life. Doctors smoked. Soldiers smoked. Smoking appeared in advertisements, restaurants, and hospitals. Some medical authorities even promoted particular brands.

When a behavior is widespread, people sometimes overlook its possible harmful effects. Experts at the British conference noticed that cities had higher lung-cancer rates than rural areas, but many blamed air pollution without seriously testing cigarette use.

This chapter asks us to distinguish between what seems plausible and what the evidence actually shows. A familiar idea, such as pollution causing lung illness, may draw attention away from another possibility.

Austin Bradford Hill recruits Richard Doll

The British Medical Research Council turned to Austin Bradford Hill, a leading biostatistician who had helped establish more rigorous methods for medical trials. Hill enlisted Richard Doll, a young researcher with limited experience in studies of this scale.

The project began with remarkably little money. Yet Hill and Doll possessed something more important than a large laboratory: a method for comparing exposures between groups of people.

They planned to interview people with lung cancer and compare their histories with patients hospitalized for other conditions. Rather than decide the answer in advance, they asked about many possible exposures, including diet, housing, industrial surroundings, and smoking.

Across the Atlantic, Ernst Wynder notices a clue

At about the same time, an American medical student named Ernst Wynder encountered the body of a patient who had died from lung cancer. He noticed the heavy evidence of long-term smoking in the lungs and wondered if cigarettes were implicated.

Wynder began working with surgeon Evarts Graham, already an important figure in lung surgery. Graham had initially been skeptical of a smoking connection, but he was willing to participate in a systematic investigation.

These two research efforts developed independently. That later mattered enormously: when different teams, populations, and methods produce compatible results, the findings become harder to dismiss as an accident of one study.

Understanding a case-control study

A case-control study starts with people who already have a disease and compares them with people who do not have that disease. Researchers then investigate whether the two groups differed in earlier exposures.

For lung cancer, the logic was:

  1. Select patients with lung cancer: the cases.
  2. Select comparison patients without lung cancer: the controls.
  3. Ask both groups about previous smoking and other habits.
  4. Compare how frequently smoking occurred in the two groups.

If cigarette smoking was much more common among people with lung cancer, it would support a link between smoking and the disease. But the method must handle differences between groups and the possible inaccuracies of people's memories.

The result appears almost immediately

By May 1, 1948, Doll and Hill had received 156 interviews. When they examined the data, one association stood out: smoking. As more patients were interviewed, the relationship became stronger.

The result changed Doll's own behavior. He had previously suspected road tar and was a smoker himself. Confronted with the data, he stopped smoking during the investigation.

Wynder and Graham's American work was converging on a very similar result. In the studies described by Mukherjee, the cigarette habit was far more closely linked to lung cancer than the other explanations under consideration.

Why association is not automatically proof of cause

A case-control study looks backward after the illness has occurred. This creates possible biases. Patients may misremember an exposure, or interviewers may unconsciously ask cases more intensely about a suspicious habit than controls.

There may also be confounding factors: something associated with both smoking and disease that complicates interpretation. For example, if smokers and nonsmokers live in different environments, researchers need to investigate whether environmental differences could explain part of an observed association.

This does not make case-control studies worthless. It means that researchers need multiple kinds of evidence to argue convincingly that an exposure helps cause a disease.

An impossible perfect experiment

Hill knew that random assignment could strengthen causal conclusions. In an imaginary experiment, a large population would be assigned at random: half would smoke for years and half would not. Investigators would then compare lung-cancer outcomes.

But such a human experiment would be profoundly unethical. No researcher could justifiably force participants to smoke a potentially harmful product for decades.

A challenge arose: how could researchers study a potentially dangerous habit without deliberately causing harm?

The answer was to observe people who already made different choices, and follow them into the future.

Prospective research: watching illness develop

A prospective cohort study starts before the outcome occurs. Researchers identify a group of people, record their exposures, and follow them over time to see who becomes ill.

Imagine enrolling 10,000 people today. Some smoke; others do not. After recording their habits, we follow them for many years and count new lung-cancer cases. This avoids asking only cancer patients to reconstruct their past after diagnosis.

It is still not equivalent to random assignment: smokers and nonsmokers can differ in other ways. But prospective follow-up offers a valuable, independent source of evidence.

Inspiration from a study of moths

Mukherjee describes how Edmund Ford, a British geneticist, studied changing moth populations by marking individuals and observing them over time. Rather than relying only on fossils to infer evolution, Ford watched changes occur in living populations.

Hill realized that a comparable strategy could be used for disease. Instead of working backward from a cancer diagnosis, epidemiologists could start with a population and observe who eventually developed cancer.

The analogy is important because it illustrates a broader scientific principle: sometimes a process becomes understandable only when time is built into the study design.

The British doctors' cohort (1951)

Britain possessed a particularly useful group to follow: registered physicians, whose deaths and causes of death were more systematically recorded than those of the general public.

On October 31, 1951, Doll and Hill mailed questionnaires to roughly 59,600 doctors. The questions focused mainly on smoking habits. More than 41,000 physicians replied.

The researchers divided respondents according to their smoking histories and then tracked reported deaths and their causes. This was an unusually large and practical observational study.

The results become difficult to deny

During the approximately 29 months after enrollment described in the book, 789 deaths were recorded among the responding doctors. Thirty-six were attributed to lung cancer. All thirty-six occurred in smokers.

That finding was powerful because smoking habits had been recorded before those deaths. The researchers had not simply assembled people with lung cancer and asked afterward who smoked. They had followed an exposed and an unexposed population through time.

The study strengthened the emerging picture that cigarette smoking was a major risk factor for lung cancer. Later and larger analyses would extend that evidence further.

What two study designs contribute

Case-control study Prospective cohort study
Begins with disease status Begins with a defined population
Looks backward at previous exposure Records exposure and watches outcomes unfold
Often practical for relatively rare cancers Can estimate future disease rates across exposure groups
Vulnerable to recall and selection problems Requires time, follow-up, and attention to confounding
Helped reveal the smoking link Helped confirm its strength and timing

Both designs can be useful. They answer related questions from different directions. Independent convergence can be more persuasive than any one method alone.

Why the chapter's title matters

The title, “The Emperor's Nylon Stockings,” belongs to Mukherjee's broader portrayal of a disease wrapped in changing cultural disguises. The real investigative achievement here, however, is plain: researchers stripped away assumptions and made a familiar habit measurable as a possible cause of illness.

Science advanced not through a single miraculous microscope image, but through interviews, records, careful group comparisons, and follow-up over years.

Key terms

Term Simple meaning
Case A participant who has the disease under investigation
Control A comparison participant without that disease
Cohort A defined group followed or studied together
Prospective Looking forward from recorded exposures toward later outcomes
Retrospective Looking backward from outcomes toward earlier circumstances
Confounding When another factor complicates an apparent relationship
Bias A systematic problem that can distort a study's result

Final chapter takeaway

Doll, Hill, Wynder, and Graham demonstrated the value of well-designed epidemiology. They moved the smoking question from intuition and debate toward reproducible evidence collected from human populations.

A disease may look mysterious when each patient is considered alone. When researchers examine thousands of people and compare their exposures, a pattern that was hidden in individual stories can become unmistakable.
