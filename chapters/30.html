The mystery of rapidly increasing lung cancer

By the 1940s, doctors in Britain are alarmed by a sharp increase in lung cancer. Explanations include motor exhaust, city pollution, new diagnostic techniques, and many other features of modern life. British researchers Richard Doll and Austin Bradford Hill decide to investigate the question using systematic epidemiology rather than unsupported speculation.

Comparing cases and controls

They interview patients with lung cancer and people without it, asking about smoking habits and other exposures. Such a case-control study looks backward from disease to possible causes. If smoking is far more common among patients with lung cancer, the association deserves investigation. But backward-looking studies can be distorted by recall and selection, so the researchers seek stronger evidence.

The importance of prospective evidence

Doll and Hill follow a large group of British doctors over time, recording their smoking habits before observing who develops lung cancer. This prospective cohort study helps overcome some weaknesses of asking people to remember exposures after becoming ill. The pattern is dramatic: lung cancer deaths cluster in smokers, while nonsmokers have much lower risk.

Why the title is playful

The whimsical chapter title contrasts with the severe conclusion. A product sold as elegant and harmless is closely associated with a lethal disease. The study demonstrates how rigorous comparison can uncover dangers that are difficult to notice in one individual lifetime.

What is and is not proved

A statistical association is not identical to a full biochemical explanation. Yet a powerful, consistent association across carefully designed studies can provide strong evidence of causation. The chapter lays the foundation for the political fight over what society should do when the evidence points toward a highly profitable product.
