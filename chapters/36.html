A controversial treatment becomes an industry

High-dose chemotherapy with marrow or stem-cell support spreads rapidly in the 1990s, particularly among women with advanced or high-risk breast cancer. Hospitals build transplant units, insurers face pressure to pay, and patients sometimes demand access even before definitive trials are complete. A treatment still being evaluated begins to look like a standard of care.

Desperation changes the politics of evidence

Women facing life-threatening disease fear being denied the treatment that might save them. Activists, physicians, insurance companies, and courts become involved. The emotional force of a possible cure makes randomized trials harder to conduct, because many patients are unwilling to be assigned to a less intensive treatment arm.

The Bezwoda controversy

South African oncologist Werner Bezwoda reports striking findings that seem to support high-dose therapy. His data acquire enormous influence while other studies offer much less encouraging results. Investigations later uncover grave problems with the reliability of his research. The episode shows how scientific error or misconduct can become particularly damaging when it supports a treatment that patients desperately want to believe in.

The trials change the conclusion

Large studies ultimately fail to demonstrate the dramatic survival advantage promised for the high-dose approach in breast cancer, while serious toxicity remains. The chapter traces the unraveling of a powerful therapeutic fashion. It does not deny the real achievements of chemotherapy; it insists that each proposed escalation must earn its place through evidence.

The map and the parachute

The title points toward a dangerous dilemma: how much knowledge is necessary before taking a life-or-death leap? The chapter argues that sound trials are not an obstacle to compassionate care; they are how medicine learns whether an intervention is worth its risks. Having pursued drugs to an extreme, the book now turns inward to the mechanisms of the cancer cell itself.
