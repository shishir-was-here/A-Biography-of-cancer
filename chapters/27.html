A question left behind by tamoxifen

In Chapter 26, Mary (Moya) Cole showed that tamoxifen could shrink some metastatic breast cancers. But the responses raised a new possibility.

What if doctors gave a useful cancer drug before the cancer had widely spread, or just after surgery had removed the visible tumor?

In advanced cancer, doctors may see numerous malignant deposits throughout the body. In earlier-stage disease, a surgeon may remove the main tumor while leaving behind cells so small that no scan or examination can detect them.

These hidden cells can eventually cause relapse. Cole wondered whether a medicine capable of suppressing visible cancer might be even more useful when the amount of remaining disease was tiny.

This idea marked a change in strategy: rather than waiting for measurable metastatic cancer, treat the possibility of microscopic disease before it becomes clinically visible.

How Halsted's ambition was reborn

William Halsted had also wanted to prevent breast cancer from returning. He believed that removing ever more tissue—muscle, lymph nodes, and surrounding structures—would eliminate invisible reservoirs of cancer.

But his method could not reach malignant cells already traveling through the blood or growing in distant organs.

Mukherjee suggests that Halsted may have had a legitimate goal but the wrong instrument. A surgical knife acts locally. A drug carried throughout the body has the potential to reach cells that surgery cannot.

This kind of treatment is called adjuvant therapy: additional treatment given after a primary treatment, such as surgery, to reduce the risk of recurrence.

An easy analogy: imagine pulling the main weed out of a garden. Visible removal may leave tiny seeds or roots elsewhere. An effective follow-up strategy aims to stop those remaining sources from growing into new weeds. The analogy is imperfect—cancer drugs have real side effects—but it helps explain the purpose.

Paul Carbone and Min Chiu Li's influence

In 1963, an NCI oncologist named Paul Carbone began exploring chemotherapy after surgery for women with early breast cancer. The visible tumor had been removed; the question was whether additional drugs could reduce later relapse.

Carbone's thinking was influenced by Min Chiu Li, the researcher who had continued treating placental tumors with methotrexate even after visible disease appeared to vanish. Li had suspected that microscopic cancer might still remain.

The book reminds us that Li had faced institutional punishment for that approach. Yet the deeper idea—that invisible malignant cells might need additional treatment—became increasingly influential.

Carbone's small initial study suggested that chemotherapy after surgery could reduce relapse. But he struggled to recruit women, because breast surgeons controlled most of these patients' care and often distrusted chemotherapists.

A major obstacle: Specialists competing for control

The conflict among cancer specialists now became a practical barrier to research.

Many surgeons considered breast cancer their domain. They thought surgery was the definitive treatment and chemotherapy was mainly for patients whose disease was already advanced.

They were therefore reluctant to cooperate with studies suggesting that surgical cures might be improved by drugs.

Bernard Fisher, who was already struggling to organize a trial questioning radical mastectomy, understood the importance of Carbone's proposal. But he could hardly persuade surgeons to join several disruptive studies at once.

The most important early trial of adjuvant chemotherapy would therefore happen somewhere unexpected: Italy.

The irony was striking. The United States had declared a national War on Cancer, yet its specialists' rivalries made it difficult to run one of the crucial collaborative trials at home.

Gianni Bonadonna and Umberto Veronesi in Milan

In 1972, Italian oncologist Gianni Bonadonna visited the NCI and learned about a combination of three drugs thought to be effective against breast cancer cells.

Bonadonna worked at a large cancer institute in Milan and maintained a productive relationship with breast surgeon Umberto Veronesi. Unlike many rivals in America, the surgeon and medical oncologist were willing to work together.

The proposed drug combination was known as CMF:

Letter Drug Basic purpose in the combination
C Cyclophosphamide (Cytoxan) A DNA-damaging chemotherapy drug
M Methotrexate An antifolate interfering with folate-dependent cell processes
F Fluorouracil (5-FU) Interferes with processes needed to make and use genetic material

The idea was to administer CMF after surgery to women with early breast cancer, hoping to eliminate microscopic disease that might later produce metastases.

The Milan trial begins (1973)

Bonadonna launched the randomized study in 1973. By late that year, roughly 400 women had entered the trial. The groups were compared according to whether they received CMF after surgery or no additional chemotherapy.

For participating patients, this was not an abstract scientific question. They had already undergone treatment and now had to decide whether to accept toxic medicines even though no cancer was visible.

A drug given after surgery may produce side effects immediately, while its main benefit—preventing a future relapse—may not be apparent for years.

The design of the trial mattered. Without a comparable group of women who received no adjuvant chemotherapy, doctors could not know how many of the treated women would have remained disease-free anyway.

The surprising results (1975)

In 1975, Bonadonna presented early results at a European cancer conference in Brussels.

After approximately two years, the groups had begun to differ substantially. The book reports that nearly half the women without additional treatment had relapsed, compared with about one-third of the women who received CMF.

That was a reduction of roughly one relapse among every six women treated in the study's early follow-up.

Group in the historical trial Approximate relapse proportion reported
Surgery without adjuvant CMF Nearly 1 in 2
Surgery followed by CMF About 1 in 3

The conference audience was stunned. Treatment after removal of the visible cancer had reduced the chance of the disease returning.

These are historical results from a specific study, not present-day personalized breast-cancer risk estimates.

Bernard Fisher asks whether tamoxifen can do the same

Bonadonna's result prompted a related question. If chemotherapy could reduce relapses after surgery, could tamoxifen reduce relapse in women whose tumors were estrogen-receptor positive?

Fisher organized a large study beginning in January 1977. The book describes 1,891 women whose cancers expressed estrogen receptors and had spread to regional axillary nodes. Some received tamoxifen after surgery, while others did not.

By 1981, the results were striking. Adjuvant tamoxifen had reduced the cancer relapse rate by nearly 50 percent in the studied group.

The benefit was particularly pronounced among women over fifty. Later follow-up reinforced the improvement in outcomes.

What made this approach distinctive was its selectivity. The drug worked best when the tumor possessed a relevant biological feature: the estrogen receptor.

Two forms of precision emerge

By the early 1980s, cancer medicine had made two different advances:

Precision by stage: Doctors recognized that a small amount of disease remaining after surgery may be more treatable than widespread metastatic cancer. The timing and stage of treatment mattered.

Precision by biology: Doctors recognized that cancers with estrogen receptors behaved differently from cancers without them. A medicine that targets hormone signaling should be tested in the people most likely to benefit.

Question How it changes treatment
Has the cancer spread? Helps determine whether local treatment is sufficient and whether additional systemic treatment is appropriate
Could microscopic cells remain? May support adjuvant treatment after surgery
Does the tumor have estrogen receptors? Helps identify patients who may benefit from anti-estrogen treatment
What does treatment add to survival or recurrence risk? Requires careful comparison in clinical trials

This was a departure from the fantasy that all cancers could be treated with one aggressive formula.

Progress does not mean certainty

These discoveries improved cancer care, but Mukherjee avoids presenting adjuvant therapy as a universal cure. Not all patients benefited, and some eventually relapsed despite treatment.

Breast cancer was proving heterogeneous, meaning it contained many biologically different forms. Some tumors grew slowly; others spread rapidly. Some responded to estrogen blockade; others did not. Some patients had truly local disease, while others had microscopic dissemination long before diagnosis.

This complexity required scientists to replace the simple idea of one cure with more modest, more specific advances.

The historical metaphor of hitting a home run gave way to accepting a series of important smaller victories. A treatment that prevents some relapses can still matter enormously, even if it cannot eliminate cancer everywhere.

“More is more”: A physician confronts a family

Mukherjee now shifts from historical research to his own experience caring for patients.

He describes an elderly Italian woman with advanced liver cancer that had spread widely through her abdomen. She was exhausted and increasingly fragile. Her liver and kidneys were functioning poorly.

Her daughter, herself a physician, wanted the strongest treatments available—chemotherapy, radiation, surgery, perhaps all three. She was deeply devoted to her mother and believed that more aggressive intervention meant better care.

Mukherjee worried that the woman could not tolerate a powerful regimen. He suggested a gentler, symptom-focused approach instead of attempting to cure an incurable disease with treatment her organs might not survive.

The daughter rejected the idea. Eventually, the family sought another doctor. Mukherjee later admits that he did not know whether the woman died of her cancer or of the treatment given against it.

The story asks a difficult question: When does an attempt to save life become an additional source of suffering?

Palliative care: A different kind of medical success

The author then introduces a field often treated as the opposite of aggressive cancer treatment: palliative medicine.

Palliative care focuses on relieving pain, nausea, breathlessness, anxiety, and other burdens of serious illness. It also supports patients and families in making decisions that reflect their priorities.

In the treatment culture described by Mukherjee, doctors frequently regarded symptom relief as evidence that they had given up the battle. They feared that acknowledging dying patients would mean acknowledging medical failure.

But a person with advanced cancer still has needs that deserve skilled medical attention. Not being able to remove a tumor does not mean there is nothing useful or compassionate to do.

Palliative care is not synonymous with abandoning treatment, and it is not limited to the final days of life. The chapter particularly emphasizes the neglected care of people approaching death.

Cecily Saunders changes end-of-life medicine

In Britain, Cecily Saunders, first a nurse and later a physician, became a central figure in the modern hospice movement.

In the late 1940s, she cared for a dying Jewish refugee from Warsaw. He left her a small sum of money, expressing a wish to become a window in a future home for people facing death.

Saunders encountered patients who were denied effective pain relief, meaningful companionship, and basic dignity simply because their illnesses could no longer be cured.

She argued that such people were not failed cases to hide away. They were patients deserving careful, specialized treatment. She drew on medicine, nursing, psychology, rehabilitation, and other disciplines to build an approach centered on comfort and dignity.

In 1967, she founded St. Christopher's Hospice in London, named for the patron saint of travelers.

Hospice spreads and old assumptions are questioned

The hospice movement slowly influenced American medicine. The book identifies an early US hospice initiative at Yale–New Haven in 1974.

Doctors began taking pain treatment and symptom research more seriously. Long-held fears about appropriate opioid use for severe cancer pain were challenged, and new anti-nausea treatments improved the experience of patients receiving chemotherapy.

This did not remove every risk associated with pain medicines. It did expose the cruelty of denying effective symptom relief because of generalized fear or prejudice.

Saunders's larger point was philosophical: medicine should not define every death as a professional defeat. A good physician sometimes helps someone live longer; at other times, the physician helps that person live more comfortably and die with dignity.

The chapter's deeper meaning

This chapter brings together two kinds of progress that initially seem unrelated.

Adjuvant therapy acknowledges that cancer may require treatment beyond what the surgeon can see. It uses the disease's stage and biology to prevent future suffering.

Palliative care acknowledges that even when cancer cannot be eliminated, patients deserve treatment for suffering that is real right now.

Both approaches reject a narrow understanding of success. Care is not measured simply by how many drugs are administered or how much tissue is removed.

Chapter takeaway: Knowing cancer means understanding its biological behavior, but caring for someone with cancer also means knowing when treatment helps—and when the most important treatment is relief, honesty, and dignity.
