Halsted's logic reappears in chemotherapy

After surgeons learn that more extensive operations are often not better, some oncologists begin to apply the same escalating logic to drug treatment. If strong chemotherapy cures some cancers, perhaps even stronger chemotherapy can eradicate hidden cells in other cancers. The chapter examines adjuvant therapy, treatment given after primary surgery to reduce the chance that unseen cancer cells will cause relapse.

The promise of treating invisible disease

Breast-cancer trials investigate combinations of surgery, radiation, hormonal therapy, and chemotherapy. The logic is medically important: removing a visible tumor does not guarantee that every malignant cell has disappeared. Additional treatment may lower the risk of recurrence, but its benefit must be measured against toxicity and tailored to risk.

No single treatment fits everyone

Trials reveal different patterns across different cancers and different patient groups. Some breast tumors respond to hormonal treatment; others do not. Some patients benefit from added chemotherapy; others face harm with less gain. The chapter deepens the theme that biological heterogeneity requires therapeutic heterogeneity.

Caring when cure is no longer possible

Mukherjee introduces Cicely Saunders, whose work helped develop modern hospice and palliative care. She recognizes that relieving pain, fear, isolation, and suffering is not an admission that medicine has no purpose. The goals of care can change without the patient becoming less deserving of attention. A good doctor must know not only when to attack a tumor, but also when to protect a person's remaining life from unnecessary suffering.

The chapter's moral center

The war metaphor can obscure the patient. A disease may be resistant to treatment, but a person is never a failed battle. Comfort, dignity, honest communication, and meaningful time are legitimate medical achievements in their own right.
