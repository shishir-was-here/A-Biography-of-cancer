PART 02 · CHAPTER 19 · An Impatient War

VAMP

PDF pages 110–1158 min reading1,556 wordsDetailed chapter

VAMP becomes one of the most daring and painful experiments in early chemotherapy. At first, its four drugs nearly destroy the children receiving them. Then the leukemia seems to vanish—until a hidden refuge in the brain causes devastating relapses. Decades later, a survivor named Ella reveals both the achievement and the human cost of this new medicine.

1. Why VAMP frightened even cancer specialists

Frei and Freireich proposed giving children vincristine, amethopterin, mercaptopurine, and prednisone as an intensive combination. Colleagues reacted with horror. Each drug was toxic on its own; the four together might destroy the healthy blood-forming cells that the children needed to survive.

The children already had dangerously fragile immune systems and low blood counts because of leukemia. A powerful regimen could worsen those problems before it had a chance to control the cancer. The doctors could potentially cause fatal infections or organ injury while trying to save their patients.

At professional meetings, Frei and Freireich were criticized as reckless or cruel. Farber himself favored a more cautious approach of adding drugs progressively. The cooperative leukemia research group refused to sponsor the proposed trial at that point.

2. The decision to proceed independently

Frei negotiated permission to test VAMP at the National Cancer Institute outside the main cooperative group. This damaged a collaboration he had helped build and placed the NCI under intense scrutiny.

Gordon Zubrod understood the danger. If the children died because of the experimental drugs, the government research institute might be accused of conducting abusive human experiments. The scientists also knew that childhood leukemia was almost invariably fatal without effective treatment.

The choice was agonizing because the risks existed on both sides. Refusing to experiment might leave the children to die of leukemia. Proceeding might expose them to death caused or hastened by treatment.

3. The VAMP trial begins in 1961

The first patients were gravely ill. Almost immediately after VAMP started, many became much worse. The drugs damaged the normal bone marrow and wiped out healthy blood-cell production.

Some children became so weak that they required mechanical breathing support. Others faced severe infections while unable to mount a normal immune response. Doctors who had argued against VAMP saw the catastrophe they had feared taking shape.

Freireich remained close to the patients. He visited repeatedly, adjusted supportive care, and worried constantly about their condition. When medicine offered no immediate way to reverse the side effects, he relied on ordinary acts of caregiving: comfort, blankets, attention, and reassurance.

That image matters. The same doctor who had advocated extraordinary doses of experimental chemicals was forced back to the oldest duty of medicine—care for the person in the bed.

4. Then the marrow begins to recover

After about three excruciating weeks, some children survived the deepest period of toxicity. Their healthy blood cells slowly began returning.

Meanwhile, the leukemia was no longer visible in bone-marrow examinations. Additional tests also failed to reveal malignant cells. These were extraordinarily deep remissions compared with the brief responses previously seen with single drugs.

The sequence seemed almost impossible: first, chemotherapy devastated the marrow; then, healthy blood production recovered while the leukemia remained suppressed. It was as though the treatment had cleared a crowded factory of faulty production and made room for normal production to begin again.

The analogy has a limit, however. The absence of visible leukemia in marrow samples does not prove every malignant cell in the body is gone.

5. Early success changes the mood at the NCI

By 1962, Frei and Freireich had treated a small initial group with VAMP and observed prolonged remissions. More children were returning to the clinic after repeated cycles of therapy, some wearing scarves or wigs while their hair grew back.

Other hospitals cautiously began using the approach. Physicians who had once considered aggressive combination chemotherapy unacceptable started reconsidering it. The mood among leukemia doctors shifted from the expectation of almost certain death toward the possibility of durable disease control.

But the optimism was still based on a small number of patients. The full course of the disease had not yet revealed itself.

6. A shocking new pattern: Headaches and neurological problems

Some children who had seemed to recover returned with headaches, unusual sensations, or visual disturbances. Doctors tested their bone marrow and found no obvious recurrence there.

Then they examined the cerebrospinal fluid, the liquid surrounding the brain and spinal cord. It contained leukemia cells.

The cancer had found a place the drugs had not adequately reached. Children who appeared to have achieved systemic remissions developed leukemia in the nervous system. Some progressed to coma and died.

This was a devastating discovery. The treatment had altered the disease's apparent pattern without eliminating the danger.

7. The blood–brain barrier becomes a hiding place

The blood–brain barrier is a protective cellular system that restricts entry of many substances from blood into the brain. Its ordinary function is beneficial: it helps shield sensitive nervous tissue from harmful substances.

But some chemotherapy drugs cannot easily cross it. If leukemia cells enter the nervous system while circulating drugs fail to reach them, the brain and spinal fluid can become a sanctuary site for residual disease.

Think of a castle whose walls keep poisonous weapons out. If the enemy has already slipped inside, those walls may inadvertently protect the enemy as well.

The same property that protects a healthy brain can create a treatment problem. Future leukemia protocols would need to address this hidden compartment directly.

8. The emotional and institutional collapse

The relapses hit the NCI team hard. Doctors had watched the children endure almost intolerable treatment and then celebrated results they hoped were permanent. Now many of those same children were returning in worse condition.

The program's leaders were exhausted, and professional hostility remained intense. In 1963 Frei left the NCI for MD Anderson in Houston, Texas. Freireich soon followed. Their collaboration continued, but the original NCI program lost the team that had held it together.

Mukherjee offers an important correction to histories that center only on doctors. The deeper heroism, he suggests, belongs to the patients who actually endured the treatments, relapses, fear, and uncertain futures.

9. The unexpected exception: A few children survive

Not every child relapsed. Mukherjee explains that about 5% of children treated in the early VAMP programs remained in remission over the longer term.

That percentage is small, and it would be wrong to describe VAMP as a general cure. But a handful of children survived years without detectable leukemia. They grew older, returned for checkups, and gradually reentered ordinary life.

For a disease that had once killed nearly every affected child, even this limited result was historically important. It indicated that chemotherapy had crossed a new threshold: a few patients might be living proof of genuine cure.

10. Meeting Ella decades later

Years after the experiments, Mukherjee travels to Maine to meet a woman he calls Ella, a long-term survivor of childhood leukemia treated with VAMP.

Ella was diagnosed in 1964 at age eleven. Photographs from her illness show a frightening transformation: she became weak, severely underweight, and unable to walk normally. Chemotherapy caused terrible side effects.

Vincristine injured her nerves, leaving lasting burning sensations in her limbs. Prednisone contributed to frightening changes in her behavior and mental state. For severe pain she received morphine and later had to struggle through withdrawal. She remembers restraints, helplessness, and the suffering of children around her who did not survive.

Decades later, her account remains emotional. She cannot explain why she became sick—or why she was spared when others died. Her survival is neither a neat triumph nor a simple medical statistic.

11. Why Ella's story matters more than a percentage

A survival rate tells us how often an outcome occurs across a group. Ella shows us what one survival actually contains: childhood fear, long hospitalization, damaged nerves, painful memories, and a lifetime of reflecting on being alive.

The story forces us to think about quality of survival, not merely length of survival. A cure can be an enormous victory while leaving physical and psychological consequences.

It also reminds us not to turn patients into symbols without recognizing their separate lives. Their suffering is not valuable only because it advances science; it matters in itself.

12. Farber takes the survivor photographs to Washington

Farber recognized the significance of children who remained cancer-free after these harsh treatments. In 1964 he brought photographs of long-term survivors to Congress as evidence that chemical treatment could sometimes cure cancer.

For the campaign led by Farber and Mary Lasker, those photographs were immensely persuasive. They represented something that statistical charts alone could not communicate: children once considered beyond hope could grow up.

Farber now wanted to apply the same logic to other cancers—breast, lung, kidney, ovarian, and others. Whether that could work was still unknown. The lesson from VAMP was powerful, but it did not prove that all tumors would respond similarly.

13. The deeper meaning: Scientific hope must hold two truths

VAMP showed that intense combinations could produce dramatic remissions and, rarely at first, long-term survival. It also showed that a treatment could fail because cancer survived in anatomical sites protected from the drugs.

For doctors, it was tempting to tell a story of brave researchers pushing through skepticism. Mukherjee makes the story harder and more honest: the same strategy caused great suffering, and the main trial ended in grief for most patients.

Chapter takeaway: VAMP proves that combination chemotherapy can sometimes produce deep, lasting responses in childhood leukemia, but the discovery of nervous-system relapses shows why visible remission is not enough. Ella's survival embodies both the extraordinary achievement and the human cost of early chemotherapy.
