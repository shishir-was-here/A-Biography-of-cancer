Looking at the lung before cancer appears

Pathologist Oscar Auerbach studies lungs from many smokers and nonsmokers and documents a sequence of cellular changes in damaged tissue. His work helps connect cigarette exposure with precancerous alterations, not only fully developed tumors. This strengthens the causal story: cancer can emerge through a process of progressive biological injury rather than springing into existence all at once.

The surgeon general's warning

In 1964, the U.S. surgeon general's report formally recognizes the serious health risks of smoking. It marks a turning point in public-health communication. Cigarettes had been marketed for decades as glamorous, comforting, and socially desirable; now a prominent public authority clearly links them to disease.

Counteradvertising and public pressure

Antismoking campaigns use statistics, clinical evidence, and personal stories. Television advertisements and warning messages force the public to encounter the physical consequences of smoking. Yet the tobacco companies respond aggressively, seeking to preserve sales and defend a product with deeply established consumers.

Rose Cipollone and the courtroom

The chapter follows Rose Cipollone, a long-term smoker who develops lung cancer, and the legal battle involving cigarette manufacturers. Litigation turns scientific questions about risks, warnings, and industry conduct into public courtroom disputes. Individual responsibility becomes entangled with addiction and the companies' knowledge of the product's dangers.

A major prevention achievement with unfinished work

Smoking rates eventually fall substantially in many places, illustrating the power of evidence combined with public action. But the danger persists wherever tobacco is accessible and aggressively marketed. Mukherjee's broader point is that identifying a preventable cause can save more lives than a dramatic treatment—provided society acts on the evidence.
