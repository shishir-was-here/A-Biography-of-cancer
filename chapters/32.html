Oscar Auerbach looks at cancer before it appears

In 1963, three members of a committee appointed by the United States surgeon general visited the laboratory of lung pathologist Oscar Auerbach in New Jersey. Auerbach had examined lung tissues from 1,522 autopsies of smokers and nonsmokers.

His major contribution was to look not only for fully formed tumors, but for the precancerous changes that appeared earlier. He observed a progression: airway linings thickened, cells acquired unusual features, abnormalities increased, and in some cases cells eventually invaded tissue as cancer.

This supplied a biological story compatible with the population statistics. Smoking did not have to cause an obvious tumor overnight. It could contribute to a long, gradual process of cellular change.

The difference between cancer and precancer

A precancerous lesion is a tissue abnormality with the potential to develop into cancer. It is not automatically an invasive malignancy. Some changes remain stable or regress; others progress.

Auerbach's findings suggested that a cancer diagnosis might be the final visible stage of a much longer sequence. If doctors could identify the causes of the earliest changes, they might prevent malignant disease before it reached a dangerous stage.

This was an important bridge between the epidemiology of Part Four and the cellular biology that the book later explores in greater depth.

Medical organizations call for federal action (1961)

In 1961, three major American health organizations asked President John F. Kennedy to establish a commission to investigate tobacco and health. The request itself revealed political tension: public-health advocates wanted the evidence announced while trying not to provoke powerful tobacco interests too directly.

Kennedy passed the issue to Luther Terry, the surgeon general. Terry had grown up in Alabama, where tobacco was part of everyday life, and had spent his career near the expanding world of cancer research.

He understood that a short, unsupported public warning could be dismissed. Instead, he created a scientific advisory committee to review the accumulated evidence comprehensively.

An unusually ambitious evidence review

The ten-member committee included experts in statistics, medicine, pathology, chemistry, epidemiology, and related fields. Mukherjee notes the irony that several members were themselves smokers, and cigarette ashtrays were present during deliberations about smoking's dangers.

Over thirteen months, the committee reviewed an enormous body of research: thousands of papers, dozens of studies, laboratory findings, autopsy investigations, and population data involving more than a million people.

It evaluated results in combination rather than relying on the reputation of a single investigator. The statistician William Cochran examined ways to synthesize findings across separate studies—an approach related to what later became known as meta-analysis.

The surgeon general's report (January 11, 1964)

The committee concluded that cigarette smoking was a major cause of lung cancer. The evidence was strikingly consistent across different populations and kinds of investigation.

On January 11, 1964, Luther Terry released the official surgeon general's report. Its significance was not that scientists had suddenly made an entirely new discovery. Researchers such as Doll, Hill, Wynder, and Graham had already provided strong evidence.

The achievement was public and institutional: an American government health authority was now putting its weight behind a conclusion that the tobacco industry had spent years questioning.

Why a government statement mattered

A research paper usually reaches a limited scientific audience. An official government report can reach journalists, legislators, schools, doctors, and ordinary citizens.

It also changes the political conversation. The question moves from “Is this association real?” toward “What should society do about the risk?”

But the report did not immediately end cigarette use, advertising, or political resistance. The tobacco industry remained powerful, and regulatory authority in America was fragmented. Evidence had to be translated into laws, public warnings, and changing behavior.

The fight over cigarette-package warnings

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) wanted stronger warning requirements and greater control over misleading cigarette advertising. Tobacco interests opposed stringent federal action.

Mukherjee describes an unexpected political tactic: cigarette companies sought regulation through Congress, which they judged more sympathetic to industry, rather than allowing the FTC to impose stricter rules.

Congress passed the Federal Cigarette Labeling and Advertising Act in 1965. Packages were required to carry a health warning, but the legislation was also crafted in a way that constrained some stronger regulatory proposals. This was a partial public-health victory, not a decisive one.

John Banzhaf challenges television advertising

A law student named John Banzhaf questioned why broadcasters could devote so much airtime to cigarette promotions without providing comparable time for opposing public-health information.

He appealed to the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), invoking the fairness doctrine governing broadcasters' treatment of controversial public issues. The complaint ultimately contributed to the appearance of antismoking messages on television.

The important development was that tobacco companies could no longer entirely control the televised story of smoking. Viewers were able to encounter messages showing the harm rather than only images of glamour or relaxation.

The end of cigarette commercials on American television

Antismoking messages became increasingly direct. Mukherjee describes the actor William Talman, who appeared in a public-service advertisement while gravely ill with lung cancer, warning smokers to quit.

Facing changes in broadcasting policy and mounting criticism, cigarette makers withdrew from broadcast advertising. The last American television cigarette advertisement aired at the start of 1971.

This did not eliminate smoking, but it changed the media landscape. The cigarettes that had saturated entertainment and advertising could no longer be promoted in exactly the same way on television.

Statistics need human faces: Rose Cipollone

Mukherjee then focuses on Rose Cipollone, who began smoking as a teenager during World War II. She lived through decades in which cigarette companies marketed smoking as a sign of composure and freedom, particularly for women.

Her smoking became a difficult dependency. Even when family members supplied articles warning about health risks, she struggled to quit. She moved between brands, repeatedly hoping that new filters, reduced tar, or supposedly gentler formulations might make smoking safer.

Her story demonstrates the difference between knowing that a behavior is dangerous and being able to stop it—particularly when a product delivers an addictive substance.

Rose's illness and death

In 1981, Rose developed a cough. Investigation revealed a lung tumor. The cancer later spread to other organs, and chemotherapy offered little benefit.

As her disease advanced, she became increasingly debilitated and required strong pain relief. She died on October 21, 1984, at the age of fifty-eight.

Until this point, smoking's consequences could be expressed in millions of cigarettes sold, percentages of smokers, or population mortality curves. Rose's story brings those numbers down to one family and one shortened life.

An unusual lawsuit against cigarette companies

Attorney Marc Edell helped bring a lawsuit on behalf of Rose and her family against tobacco manufacturers whose products she had used. Earlier lawsuits had often failed when companies argued that consumers already knew the risks and chose to smoke anyway.

Edell's strategy was different. He did not pretend Rose was unaware of the warnings. Instead, he asked what the companies themselves had known about cancer and nicotine addiction, and whether they had concealed information from consumers.

The change in focus—from the smoker's knowledge to the industry's knowledge—opened an important legal route.

Internal documents reveal a deliberate strategy

Through litigation, lawyers obtained corporate records that showed how tobacco interests had discussed nicotine dependence, health risks, and methods for sustaining doubt.

Some documents described techniques such as acknowledging controversy without resolving it, emphasizing personal choice, and calling for additional scientific investigation while continuing to sell products.

These documents transformed the argument. The issue was no longer only whether smoking was dangerous; it was also whether commercial organizations had acted responsibly in communicating the danger.

Smoking decreases in America, but the problem moves elsewhere

The combined effects of research, warnings, social pressure, lawsuits, and changing attitudes contributed to a long decline in American smoking. The book notes a drop in per-capita cigarette consumption between the 1970s and 1990s.

But cigarette companies increasingly pursued markets in other countries, particularly those where regulation or public understanding of risk was less developed. The danger did not disappear simply because one country reduced smoking.

Mukherjee points to the major burden of tobacco-related deaths in countries such as India and China. Prevention must be considered globally, not only in the countries where the early studies and lawsuits occurred.

What this chapter teaches about public-health success

Scientific step Social or political step
Identify a convincing risk factor Inform people clearly about that risk
Study tissue changes and biological mechanisms Regulate misleading promotion and unsafe marketing
Combine evidence from many populations Require health warnings and support behavior change
Measure how exposure patterns change Challenge concealment and hold institutions accountable
Track cancer outcomes over decades Keep prevention efforts active across countries

The table illustrates why a scientific conclusion alone may not prevent disease. A carcinogen can remain widely available even when its harms are well understood.

Key terms

Surgeon general: A senior public-health official who can communicate official conclusions and health advice. Meta-analysis: Combining evidence from multiple studies to obtain a broader estimate. Precancer: Abnormal cellular change that can precede malignancy. Addiction: A condition involving compulsive substance use despite harmful consequences. Public-health regulation: Rules intended to reduce risks across a population.

The deeper meaning and final takeaway

This chapter is about the difficult journey from scientific truth to social action. Studies made the smoking–cancer link increasingly clear. But powerful industries, complicated laws, advertising, dependency, and political reluctance delayed the response.

Rose Cipollone's story prevents the narrative from becoming only a history of government agencies. The consequences of inaction were experienced in ordinary people's bodies and families.

Cancer prevention requires more than discovering what is dangerous. It requires communicating evidence, changing environments, confronting economic incentives, and helping people avoid or escape harmful exposure.
