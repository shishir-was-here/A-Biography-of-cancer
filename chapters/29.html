An unexpected clue in eighteenth-century London

In 1775, surgeon Percivall Pott notices scrotal cancer appearing disproportionately among chimney sweeps, particularly boys who spend their childhood crawling through soot-covered chimneys. Instead of viewing every tumor as random misfortune, he asks what the affected patients have in common. The work becomes an early landmark in the idea that something in a person's environment or occupation can cause cancer.

Soot, exposure, and prevention

Pott does not know the chemical mechanism, but he recognizes that prolonged soot contact is dangerous. The observation changes the question: perhaps some cancers can be prevented by avoiding the exposure that produces them. Cleaning practices and working conditions now matter to cancer medicine just as much as scalpels and medicines do.

Tobacco spreads through society

The chapter traces the rise of smoking and the enormous commercial appeal of tobacco. From pipes and snuff to factory-made cigarettes, tobacco becomes an everyday commodity woven into work, leisure, fashion, and social identity. Its danger remains comparatively obscure partly because lung cancer often appears decades after exposure. The long delay hides the causal relationship from individual observers.

The cigarette century

Cigarette production, marketing, and wartime distribution spread the habit widely. By the time researchers identify the scale of smoking's contribution to lung cancer, tens of millions of people have already been exposed. The chapter prepares a central irony of prevention: the best medical intervention may be a change in behavior or environment long before any patient develops a tumor.
