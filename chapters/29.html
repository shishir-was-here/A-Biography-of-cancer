An unusual pattern in a London hospital (1775)

The chapter begins long before modern chemotherapy or cancer genetics. In 1775, Percivall Pott, a surgeon at St. Bartholomew's Hospital in London, kept seeing patients with an unusual cancer of the scrotal skin. Rather than treating each case as unrelated bad luck, he noticed something connecting the patients: many were chimney sweeps. This was a rare cancer occurring unusually often among people who performed the same occupation.

At first Pott's professional instinct was surgical. Could he invent a better operation to remove the affected tissue? But the repeating pattern forced him to ask an earlier question: why were so many chimney sweeps developing this particular cancer? That change of question—away from treatment and toward cause—is the real beginning of Part Four.

The frightening lives of climbing-boys

Many of the chimney sweeps of eighteenth-century England were children, often impoverished or orphaned boys sold or apprenticed to master sweeps. Some were only four or five years old. They climbed narrow, soot-filled chimneys and scraped out deposits of ash in confined spaces. They could become trapped, injured, suffocated, or permanently covered with grime.

Mukherjee begins the chapter with William Blake's poem about child chimney sweeps because the medical discovery is inseparable from exploitation. Pott's patients were not people exposed to an unusual laboratory chemical by accident; they were children and young workers doing dangerous labor in an industrial society.

Their skin remained in prolonged contact with soot. The work and living conditions made regular cleaning difficult. A disease that appeared years later was therefore linked to an ordinary, repeated feature of their work.

Why Pott rejected the easy explanation

At the time, sores of the genitals were often assumed to be sexually transmitted infections. Doctors might prescribe mercury compounds, which were themselves harmful. Pott noticed that the suspicious sores did not behave as expected and that the pattern was too concentrated in one profession to ignore.

He observed that soot particles collected and remained lodged against the skin, and that the cancer sometimes appeared after a lesion workers called a soot wart. He suspected that prolonged contact with soot itself helped produce the disease.

Pott did not have a microscope capable of revealing the genetic damage we understand today. He could not identify individual carcinogenic molecules. What he could identify was an association between occupation, exposure, and a specific cancer, supported by observation of many patients.

The connection to Bernardino Ramazzini

Pott's reasoning followed an earlier tradition begun by the Italian physician Bernardino Ramazzini. In 1713, Ramazzini published a book describing illnesses that appeared more often in particular occupations. Today we call these occupational diseases.

A baker may face respiratory problems from flour dust; a worker handling certain industrial substances may face chemical hazards. Ramazzini's achievement was to insist that a patient's work mattered medically. Pott took that insight a step further: a particular material encountered at work might cause a particular cancer.

This approach is the foundation of occupational epidemiology—studying how jobs and environments affect patterns of illness.

What is a carcinogen?

A carcinogen is an agent or exposure that can cause cancer or increase the probability that it develops. It might be a chemical, a physical agent such as certain kinds of radiation, or a biological agent such as some infections.

We now know that soot can contain cancer-causing compounds. But Pott's importance was conceptual rather than molecular. He helped doctors recognize that cancer need not be explained only by mysterious internal bodily imbalances. Something outside the body could help initiate it.

This was a challenge to the long-lasting influence of Galen's black-bile theory, discussed earlier in the book. If cancer were merely the outward expression of a hidden imbalance spread through the whole body, external exposure would not be an obvious place to search for a cause.

From a cause to a possible prevention

Pott's discovery implied a second, more hopeful idea. If repeated contact with soot contributed to cancer, then reducing or removing that contact might prevent some cases.

Consider two strategies. A treatment strategy waits for a sweep to develop a malignant tumor and then tries to remove it. A prevention strategy changes the dangerous working conditions before the tumor appears. The first rescues an affected individual when possible; the second tries to reduce how many people become affected in the first place.

This is the central theme of Part Four: sometimes the most effective way to fight cancer is to prevent it from occurring.

Why prevention was so difficult

Identifying the hazard did not mean society immediately removed it. Britain relied heavily on coal, chimneys, factories, and the labor of poor children. Powerful economic interests benefited from the existing system.

Laws and enforcement developed slowly. The book recounts reforms in 1788, 1834, 1840, and 1875 that progressively restricted or abolished the practice of sending young children up chimneys. Pott himself died in 1788, before the full effect of these changes could be seen.

Over time, the occupational epidemic of scrotal cancer among sweeps receded. The lesson is not that a single surgeon personally eliminated the disease. It is that medical observation, social activism, laws, and enforcement can combine to prevent exposure.

Another warning that medicine ignored: John Hill

Mukherjee then introduces John Hill, a London apothecary and amateur naturalist. In 1761, years before Pott's publication, Hill warned in a pamphlet that using snuff, a form of tobacco, might contribute to cancers of the mouth, lips, and throat.

His claim was based on the observed connection between a repeated habit and disease. Like Pott, he lacked molecular proof. But Hill was regarded as an eccentric, self-promoting outsider to respectable medicine, so his argument attracted ridicule rather than sustained scientific investigation.

The contrast is revealing: medical authority and social reputation can influence which observations are taken seriously. An idea may deserve investigation even when its author is not part of an established institution.

Tobacco becomes a global habit

While the warning about tobacco faded, tobacco consumption expanded. In Britain, people smoked pipes and cigars or used snuff in public gathering places. Tobacco cultivation in American colonies generated enormous commercial rewards.

The cigarette made smoking more convenient and portable. The book describes how paper-wrapped tobacco spread among soldiers in the Crimean War, although the familiar tale of a soldier inventing the cigarette is probably legendary. Soldiers carried smoking habits home, and the practice spread socially across borders.

Later, mass production and advertising helped cigarettes become part of daily life. Smoking could symbolize sophistication, rebellion, masculinity, social belonging, or comfort. The same product was able to acquire different meanings for different groups.

The staggering expansion in consumption

Mukherjee gives historical numbers that make the change visible. In 1870, American cigarette consumption averaged fewer than one cigarette per person annually. Around thirty years later, Americans consumed billions of cigarettes every year. By the early 1950s, the average annual consumption had reached approximately 3,500 cigarettes per person.

These historical figures do not describe every individual's behavior, but they illustrate a mass cultural shift. A risk that had once been restricted to a relatively small group of users was becoming a widespread population exposure.

The underlying danger could remain unnoticed for decades because lung cancer often develops after long periods of exposure. A habit can become normal well before its long-term harms become obvious.

Why the connection took so long to become clear

Pott's chimney-sweep cancer involved a relatively rare occupation and a distinctive cancer, making the pattern easier to recognize. Cigarette smoking was more complicated: it spread gradually, exposed enormous populations, and was associated with a disease that might not appear until many years later.

When an exposure becomes common, people may stop noticing it as a possible explanation. If nearly everyone in a social group smokes, a doctor may regard smoking as ordinary background behavior rather than as an unusual risk factor.

This is why epidemiology needs comparison groups and measurements, not only individual stories. The next chapter will show how researchers eventually made the smoking–lung cancer relationship statistically unmistakable.

Why is the poem about coffins important?

Blake's image of children trapped in dark coffins links the physical darkness of chimneys to the hidden costs of industrial life. The workers were expected to perform dangerous labor that made other people's homes comfortable. Their suffering remained easy for the wider society to ignore.

Mukherjee uses the poem to remind us that the origins of cancer can be embedded in social arrangements. A child born into poverty may face exposures that a wealthier child can avoid. Preventing illness may require changing institutions and working conditions, not merely advising individual patients.

Important terms

Term Meaning in simple language
Carcinogen An agent or exposure that can cause or increase the risk of cancer
Occupational cancer Cancer related to harmful exposures encountered at work
Epidemiology The study of how diseases are distributed and what factors influence them in populations
Exposure Contact with a possible risk factor, such as soot or tobacco smoke
Prevention Steps taken to reduce the chance that disease will develop
Latency A delay between a harmful exposure and a disease becoming apparent

The deeper meaning of the chapter

Pott's achievement was not finding a perfect cure; it was realizing that a pattern of illness can be evidence of an external cause. That realization expanded cancer medicine beyond the operating theater and laboratory into workplaces, laws, markets, and everyday habits.

The story of John Hill adds another warning: discoveries can be overlooked when the messenger lacks prestige. The rise of tobacco then demonstrates the opposite problem: an increasingly popular and profitable habit can overwhelm early concern about its dangers.

Final chapter takeaway

The chapter moves through a simple but revolutionary sequence: repeated cancer cases among sweeps → shared exposure to soot → suspicion of a cancer-causing agent → attempts to remove the dangerous exposure → prevention of future disease.

Cancer research does not always have to begin by asking how to kill a cancer cell. Sometimes it should begin by asking why that cell became cancerous—and whether the cause can be removed.
