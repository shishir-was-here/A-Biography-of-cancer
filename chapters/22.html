The Cart and the Horse

PDF pages 131–1378 min reading1,678 wordsDetailed chapter

By the late 1960s, chemotherapy's successes create enormous optimism. Farber and the Laskerites begin to speak as if the cure for cancer is almost within reach. But two simple theories—viruses as the cause and cytotoxic drugs as the cure—are being generalized far beyond what evidence supports. The moon landing then gives the movement its most powerful political metaphor.

1. Success changes the mood of cancer medicine

By 1968, oncology had accumulated achievements that were unimaginable when Sidney Farber began his leukemia experiments. Some children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia were surviving for years after treatment. Intensive chemotherapy had also cured a significant proportion of patients in an advanced Hodgkin lymphoma study.

These were real advances. But they encouraged a much larger claim: perhaps researchers had found a general solution to cancer, and they now needed only enough money, organization, and determination to apply it everywhere.

Mukherjee uses the chapter title “The Cart and the Horse” to question this assumption. Were physicians racing toward treatments before understanding the mechanisms of the disease? Could the cart of applied treatment pull the horse of basic scientific discovery?

2. Jimmy's symbolic coming of age

Farber celebrated the Jimmy Fund's twenty-first year with a large public event in September 1968. The gathering brought together donors, politicians, doctors, and advocates who had helped build the childhood cancer campaign.

The original Jimmy, Einar Gustafson, did not appear. By then he was a grown man living privately in Maine with his family. Farber protected his anonymity, preferring that the figure of “Jimmy” remain a symbol of children with cancer rather than a permanent public spectacle focused on one man.

Farber used the occasion to celebrate something larger than a birthday: a community had formed around a once-silent disease. Fundraisers, researchers, hospitals, entertainers, and political allies were now joined by a common cause.

The party revealed how much had changed since Farber's isolated basement laboratory. Cancer was not merely discussed in medical conferences; it had become a public campaign with powerful social support.

3. The temptation to imagine one cure for all cancers

The success of high-dose combination chemotherapy was intoxicating. Researchers had discovered that carefully chosen mixtures of toxic drugs could sometimes eliminate cancers that had already spread through the body.

But two successful forms of cancer do not represent every form of cancer. Childhood leukemia and Hodgkin lymphoma have particular biological characteristics. Pancreatic, lung, and many other cancers may respond very differently.

The Laskerites nevertheless hoped that cancer could be understood as one great problem. If it were one disease with one underlying cause, perhaps a single organized national effort would find its cure.

That simplified language was politically effective, but scientifically risky. The diversity of cancers could not be removed just by giving them the same name.

4. Peyton Rous and the strange chicken tumor

Mukherjee shifts to an older scientific story. In 1909, researcher Peyton Rous at the Rockefeller Institute studied a tumor found in a chicken. It was a sarcoma, a cancer arising from connective tissue.

At first, Rous tried transferring cancer cells from one chicken to another, which could reproduce the illness. Then he filtered the tumor material through barriers that removed the cells.

The astonishing result was that the remaining filtered material could still transmit the cancer to another chicken. Something much smaller than a whole cancer cell was carrying the disease.

Rous concluded that the responsible agent was a virus. It later became known as the Rous sarcoma virus (RSV).

This was a profound discovery: at least some cancers could be triggered by infectious agents. But proving that one virus caused one animal cancer was not proof that all human cancers were viral.

5. The viral theory gains followers

Other animal tumor viruses were discovered, including viruses associated with tumors in rabbits and leukemias in certain animals. Researchers began searching for human examples.

A major development concerned Burkitt lymphoma, a cancer observed among children in parts of Africa. Its geographical distribution suggested that infection might be involved. Later work connected the lymphoma to Epstein–Barr virus (EBV).

This finding was important evidence that some human cancers had viral associations. Still, the known human examples were limited. The vast majority of cancers had not been explained by a virus.

Some scientists nevertheless became increasingly confident that viruses must offer the universal answer. The popularity of the theory grew faster than the strength of evidence for its universal application.

6. Viral causation versus the somatic mutation hypothesis

Scientists also had another broad explanation: somatic mutation. This proposed that cancer emerged when alterations occurred within the cells of the body, possibly following exposure to radiation or chemicals.

Evidence had long linked certain exposures with cancer. Chimney soot was associated with scrotal cancer among chimney sweeps. Radiation could damage tissues and later lead to cancers. Yet researchers still could not explain precisely how these different exposures produced malignant growth.

Viral hypothesis Somatic mutation hypothesis
Infection with an agent can contribute to cancer Acquired changes inside a cell contribute to malignant transformation
Supported by examples such as RSV and virus-associated tumors Supported by associations with radiation and chemical carcinogens
Could not explain most cancers on the evidence available then Lacked a full molecular explanation at that stage

The book presents the historical rivalry, not a modern either–or verdict. Later discoveries would help connect viral mechanisms and changes to cellular genes rather than requiring scientists to choose one universal explanation over the other.

7. Rous receives the Nobel Prize—and overextends his theory

In 1966, decades after his original experiments, Peyton Rous was awarded the Nobel Prize. He used the occasion to defend the importance of viruses and to criticize the mutation hypothesis.

His achievement deserved recognition, but Mukherjee emphasizes the danger of allowing a great discovery to turn into a scientific dogma. Having proven that a virus could cause one kind of cancer, some researchers acted as though viruses explained the entire disease category.

Meanwhile, others acted as though combining enough cytotoxic drugs would cure every cancer. One group offered an apparently universal cause; another offered an apparently universal treatment.

Both were attractive stories because they made a complicated problem sound simple. Both were premature.

8. Why the cart might be ahead of the horse

The title captures a central conceptual conflict. If doctors do not understand the cause and mechanisms of cancer, how reliably can they design effective treatments for all its forms?

Farber pointed to clinical successes achieved before a complete molecular explanation. His critics argued that the success of selected regimens could not be extrapolated indefinitely. Powerful therapies had already shown sharp limits, and universal viral theories had not explained most cancers.

The deeper answer is that both forms of work matter. Practical treatment can produce useful results before full understanding, but fundamental biology may be necessary to develop safer and more precise therapies.

The error lies in claiming either side is sufficient in every circumstance.

9. Solomon Garb offers a national plan

In 1968, pharmacologist Solomon Garb published Cure for Cancer: A National Goal. He argued that cancer research had suffered from chronic underfunding and insufficient organization. His proposal aligned closely with Mary Lasker's ambitions.

The Laskerites embraced Garb's book as evidence that their political agenda had scientific support. They wanted a national mission with defined responsibilities, funding, and visible results.

Mukherjee compares the movement's growing certainty to a religious crusade. It had influential advocates, a promised future, and a text that set out the mission. What it still needed was a dramatic public symbol capable of convincing the nation that such a mission could succeed.

10. July 20, 1969: Humanity reaches the Moon

The Apollo 11 Moon landing supplied that symbol. Its success seemed to prove that a rich nation could select an impossibly difficult scientific goal, dedicate resources to it, and accomplish it within a defined period.

For the Laskerites, the analogy was irresistible. If the United States could put a human being on the Moon, surely it could organize a comparable assault on cancer.

But the analogy concealed a major difference. Engineers could build Apollo using a deep foundation of physics and established technical principles. Researchers did not yet understand the central mechanisms that turned a normal cell into a malignant one.

Building a rocket using known laws is not the same as curing a disease whose governing biology remains partly unknown.

11. “Outer space” and “inner space”

Mary Lasker began describing cancer research as the conquest of inner space. The Moon project explored the space beyond Earth. The cancer project would explore the space inside the human body and defeat disease there.

The metaphor was powerful because it gave voters and politicians a clear comparison. It translated the complexity of cancer into the familiar language of national achievement.

The activists also faced political obstacles. Some long-standing allies were retiring or distracted by scandals, weakening their influence in Congress. The campaign therefore began shifting from private lobbying toward more direct public pressure.

A new message was emerging: instead of merely asking officials to support cancer research, the movement would ask ordinary Americans to demand a national cancer mission.

13. The deeper meaning: Success can produce overconfidence

One of the book's most important patterns returns here. In surgery, Halsted's ability to remove tumors encouraged the belief that ever more surgery would cure more cancer. In chemotherapy, the ability to cure some cancers encouraged the belief that drug combinations could cure them all. A real breakthrough can become dangerous when its limits are ignored.

The chapter also shows how metaphors shape policy. A “war” creates urgency, organizes people, and makes public spending seem morally necessary. But it can also suggest there is one opponent and a clear endpoint when the science is much more complicated.

Chapter takeaway: Chemotherapy and virus research produce genuine advances, but the rush toward one cause and one cure oversimplifies cancer. Apollo 11 gives the Laskerites the political image they need to demand a national “moon shot” against the disease.
