Hope outruns the clinical trial

News of early responses to Herceptin spreads among women with advanced breast cancer. Patients facing limited options ask why an experimental drug should remain confined to formal studies. The company developing it must balance access, safety, supply, regulatory obligations, and the need to gather reliable evidence.

Compassionate access versus reliable proof

An expanded-access program can help seriously ill patients receive an experimental drug outside a standard trial. But if access is broad and unstructured, it can become harder to determine who actually benefits and what adverse effects occur. The chapter shows that these are not abstract bureaucratic questions. Every choice affects people whose illness may be progressing rapidly.

Advocacy changes the process

Breast-cancer advocates, including Fran Visco, challenge the research establishment to respect patients' voices and improve access. Their involvement helps shape negotiations around availability and trial participation. The campaign is not merely for a drug, but for a different relationship between patients, pharmaceutical companies, and medical researchers.

Evidence and celebration

Clinical studies ultimately provide convincing support for Herceptin's value in selected HER2-positive breast cancers, and the treatment becomes an important example of targeted therapy. The chapter also contrasts the public celebration of a commercially successful drug with the uneven recognition of scientists whose long work made it possible.

What the story proves

Molecular medicine becomes powerful when a particular cancer feature, an appropriate drug, and a reliable clinical test are brought together. Advocacy can make development more humane, while rigorous evidence protects patients from enthusiasm that outruns reality.
