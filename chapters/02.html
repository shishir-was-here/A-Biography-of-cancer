1. A remarkable age of medical progress (1940s–1950s)

The chapter begins in an unusually optimistic period for medicine. In the years surrounding World War II, researchers were discovering medicines that could treat infections once considered deadly. Penicillin is the clearest example. During the war it was so rare that doctors tried to recover unused drug from patients' urine. In 1942, Merck's first shipment of 5.5 grams represented roughly half the American stock. Within a decade, penicillin was being manufactured in huge vats and cost only a few cents a dose.

Other antibiotics followed, including chloramphenicol in 1947 and tetracycline in 1948. Researchers made progress against tuberculosis, and at Children's Hospital in Boston John Enders was developing a laboratory method for cultivating poliovirus, an advance that contributed to later polio vaccines. By 1950, more than half of commonly used medicines had been unknown only ten years earlier.

Improved public health was equally important. Cleaner water supplies, sanitation, and better hygiene reduced the threat from diseases such as typhoid and tuberculosis. American life expectancy rose from about 47 to 68 years over half a century. Hospitals multiplied, and patients began expecting that a physician would have a specific remedy for almost any illness.

These improvements also affected society's imagination. Postwar Americans dreamed of healthy families, new homes, and lives free from the diseases that had frightened their parents. But one great medical problem had not joined this march of progress: cancer.

2. Why cancer resisted the medical revolution

Cancer was not like a bacterial infection. A physician could sometimes remove a tumor if it was confined to one accessible site. Radiation could also destroy cancer cells in a localized area. But many cancers were already spreading by the time patients reached a doctor, and some, such as leukemia, did not form a single lump that could simply be cut out.

The two major established strategies were the surgeon's knife and radiation. A surgeon removed visible malignant tissue; radiation damaged cells within a targeted region. These could cure some localized cancers, but neither was a dependable way to eliminate malignant cells scattered through multiple organs or throughout the bloodstream.

Mukherjee describes this historical choice as the cold knife and the hot ray. Both were mainly local approaches to a disease that might be systemic. Doctors desperately needed a treatment that could travel through the body and affect cancer cells wherever they were. Yet chemical chemotherapy for cancer had barely begun to exist.

Surgery Remove a reachable, localized tumor Cannot remove widely scattered malignant cells
Radiation Damage tumors in a selected region Limited ability to cure cancer that has already spread widely
Systemic cancer drugs The hoped-for future method No established broadly effective approach was available to Farber

3. Cancer rises in the American statistics

In 1899, surgeon Roswell Park predicted that cancer would eventually rival or surpass some great infectious diseases as a cause of death. Many people dismissed his concern. But as smallpox, typhoid, and tuberculosis declined, cancer became increasingly prominent. The book reports a roughly 29.8 percent rise in recorded cancer mortality from 1900 to 1916 and notes that by 1926 it had become America's second-leading killer, behind heart disease.

Part of this change was connected to longevity. When fewer people die young from infections, more survive into the ages at which cancer is common. Better detection and diagnostic accuracy also make previously hidden cases visible. The story is therefore more complicated than the idea that cancer simply appeared in modern times.

There is a striking irony: success against earlier killers made cancer more noticeable. Medical progress changed the landscape of illness, and cancer stood out as a stubborn exception.

4. “Cancer: The Great Darkness” (1937)

In May 1937, Fortune magazine published an alarming assessment of cancer research and treatment. Despite the spectacular progress of surgery and the development of X-rays, the basic treatment options had not fundamentally changed: remove malignant tissue or destroy it locally. Doctors had become better at these procedures, but they still lacked an entirely new strategy for a disease that could invade the whole body.

The article also criticized America's poor investment in cancer research. According to its estimate, funds devoted to fundamental cancer investigation had a combined capital of only a little over five million dollars. A large sporting event could consume a remarkable fraction of that sum in an afternoon. Mukherjee uses the comparison to show a mismatch between the human burden of cancer and the social resources assigned to studying it.

The darkness was therefore scientific and political. Laboratories, long-term studies, skilled researchers, and cooperative institutions needed sustained funding. A disease that concerned the nation was still receiving a fragmented and inadequate response.

5. Early efforts to organize a national response

Beginning in the early twentieth century, cancer specialists tried to persuade American politicians to support coordinated investigation. In 1907, surgeons gathered in Washington to lobby for cancer research. By 1910, they had persuaded President William Howard Taft to propose a national research laboratory. Political support was insufficient, however, and the project stalled.

A new champion arrived in the late 1920s: Senator Matthew Neely of West Virginia. Neely was concerned about the growing toll of cancer and proposed a five-million-dollar reward for information leading to a cure. The plan treated cancer almost like a criminal with a price on its head.

The response showed why the metaphor was unsuitable. Neely's office was flooded with claims about miraculous ointments, tonics, holy water, and unproven cures. Instead of producing a rigorous research program, the reward encouraged a flood of unreliable suggestions. Congress reduced the proposed budget dramatically and the effort failed to transform the field.

Deeper point: A complicated biological problem cannot necessarily be solved by offering a prize for one secret solution. It requires scientific methods, accumulated evidence, institutions, and patient work over many years.

6. The National Cancer Institute is created (1937)

Neely kept campaigning. In 1937 he joined Senator Homer Bone and Representative Warren Magnuson to renew the effort. The growing public attention to cancer gave the proposal momentum. Congress passed the legislation, and on August 5, 1937, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the National Cancer Institute Act.

The act established the National Cancer Institute (NCI) to coordinate cancer research and education. A campus was built at Bethesda, Maryland, and researchers imagined a scientifically organized campaign against the disease. For a moment, the nation's commitment seemed to be catching up with the scale of the problem.

But the timing was terrible. Europe and Asia were entering a period of escalating military conflict. Soon the United States would be drawn into a global war, and cancer research would lose much of its newly won attention.

7. World War II overshadows the war against cancer

As World War II expanded, governments reorganized their priorities. Military medicine, wartime research, troop care, and industrial production took precedence. Facilities originally imagined for cancer medicine were converted to support the war. The NCI, despite its impressive new buildings, did not receive the resources needed to achieve its ambitious goals.

What had been announced as a national effort temporarily became a quiet research institution with unused potential. The political momentum of the 1930s ebbed away. This episode illustrates the extent to which scientific progress depends on historical circumstances. A laboratory can be excellent yet accomplish little when funding, personnel, and public attention move elsewhere.

8. Cancer becomes an illness people hardly dare to name

Even after the war, cancer remained surrounded by social embarrassment and fear. Mukherjee recounts a story about Fanny Rosenow, a breast cancer survivor who tried to publicize a support-group meeting through The New York Times. According to her account, the editor refused to print the words breast or cancer and suggested a much vaguer description involving the chest wall.

The incident reveals the cultural burden of the disease. Women needed support from one another, but even naming their illness could provoke resistance. Silence could isolate patients at exactly the moment when information and companionship mattered most.

The cancer problem therefore had several layers: the biological disease, the absence of effective treatment, weak institutional support, and a public culture that often preferred not to discuss the diagnosis at all.

9. Farber decides to investigate normal blood first

Against this background, Sidney Farber was working quietly in the basement of Children's Hospital. He had chosen leukemia partly because other medical specialties had little to offer it: surgeons could not operate on circulating malignant cells, while physicians had no useful drugs. Farber approached the problem backward. Instead of beginning with what was abnormal, he wanted to learn what regulates normal blood formation.

If a chemical could switch ordinary blood-cell production on or off, perhaps a related chemical could interfere with the frantic production of malignant cells. This line of thinking brought him to earlier research in hematology—the science of blood—especially the work of George Minot and Lucy Wills.

10. George Minot and pernicious anemia

A common form of anemia results from insufficient iron, but pernicious anemia did not improve when patients were given iron. George Minot and his colleagues experimented with diets, including large quantities of liver, and showed in 1926 that a missing nutritional factor could restore blood production. The essential substance was later identified as vitamin B12. Minot shared the 1934 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with two colleagues.

The result demonstrated something conceptually powerful. Blood-cell production was not merely an automatic bodily process; it depended on specific molecules. Without the necessary nutrient, the process broke down. Supply the missing factor and the bone marrow could resume producing healthy blood.

For Farber, the implication was tantalizing: perhaps the activity of bone marrow could be controlled through chemistry.

11. Lucy Wills and the anemia of Bombay textile workers

Meanwhile, workers in the textile mills of colonial Bombay were living in severe poverty. Low wages and poor nutrition produced widespread illness, including a serious anemia affecting many women after childbirth. In 1928, British physician Lucy Wills traveled there to investigate. The condition was not corrected by the treatment that had helped Minot's patients.

Wills eventually found that a yeast-based spread called Marmite could restore normal blood production in affected patients. She could not immediately identify the active nutrient and called it the Wills factor. It was later identified as folic acid, or folate.

Mukherjee does not present the story as science detached from politics. The workers' malnutrition arose within an exploitative colonial economy. The population had been made vulnerable by social conditions and then became the subject of medical study. The discovery mattered biologically, but the circumstances behind it matter ethically.

12. Why folate matters for DNA and blood-cell production

Folate is essential for processes involved in making DNA, the genetic material a cell must copy when it divides. The bone marrow produces enormous numbers of blood cells every day, so its cells depend heavily on DNA synthesis. The chapter describes daily production on the order of hundreds of billions of cells.

Think of bone marrow as an industrial factory. To manufacture a new blood cell, its precursor must duplicate genetic instructions. Folate helps supply the ingredients needed for the duplication process. If the supply is inadequate, production stalls and immature cells accumulate. This is why deficiency can cause severe anemia.

Leukemia, however, is also a disease of cells multiplying rapidly. That meant malignant blood-forming cells might also depend on folate-supported DNA synthesis. Farber wondered whether adding folic acid would restore normal behavior to the blood system. That idea led to his first catastrophic experiment.

13. Farber's first trial becomes a dangerous failure (1946)

Farber obtained synthetic folic acid and administered it to children with leukemia. He had hoped the vitamin might help damaged bone marrow produce normal cells. Instead, the leukemia appeared to accelerate. In one child the white-cell count nearly doubled; in another, malignant cells spread aggressively into the skin. Farber quickly stopped the experiment.

Other physicians were furious, believing the treatment had worsened the children's suffering and may have hastened death. The result was not an encouraging success. It was a grave mistake with real human consequences.

But Farber recognized a disturbing biological clue: if supplying folate increased leukemia's growth, what would happen if its action were blocked? He changed the hypothesis. Rather than adding the nutrient, he wanted an antifolate—a chemical capable of interfering with folate-dependent reactions and, perhaps, malignant-cell multiplication.

14. Yellapragada Subbarao: An unlikely scientific partner

The chemist who could help Farber was Yellapragada Subbarao, an Indian physician and biochemist who had reached Boston in 1923. Without a US medical license and with little money, he initially worked as a hospital porter. Gradually he entered laboratory research and made important contributions to understanding cellular energy molecules, including ATP and creatine-related metabolism.

Despite his achievements, he struggled for recognition and tenure at Harvard. In 1940 he moved to Lederle Laboratories, where he led chemical-synthesis research. He began trying to make synthetic versions of useful biological molecules, particularly folic acid. Working with colleagues including Harriet Kiltie, he developed ways to manufacture folate and related compounds.

The chemical process yielded an unexpected possibility: slight changes in the molecule's structure produced substances that resembled folic acid but could oppose its action. The very chemistry designed to supply a vital nutrient had also created candidate antivitamins.

15. The false-key idea: How an antifolate can work

Mukherjee explains chemical specificity with a helpful analogy. In many biological reactions, enzymes and proteins recognize molecules partly by their shapes. Imagine a working molecule as a key that fits into a lock. A very similar-looking molecule may fit the same site yet interfere with the reaction—like a false key jamming a lock.

Some of Subbarao's folate-like compounds acted as antagonists in this way. This suggested a strategy for limiting the rapid cellular division characteristic of leukemia. The goal was not simply to poison everything in the bloodstream, but to interfere with a biological process on which malignant cells strongly depended.

There was still a major danger: healthy cells also require folate for normal growth. A drug that blocks these reactions can harm normal tissues, particularly rapidly dividing ones. Farber would have to discover whether an effective dose could suppress leukemia without causing intolerable injury to the patient.

16. The first package is sent to Boston

Farber wrote to Subbarao and Kiltie asking whether he could try their new antifolate compounds in children with leukemia. Subbarao agreed. In the late summer of 1947, a first package traveled from Lederle Laboratories in New York to Farber's laboratory in Boston.

The chapter brings together several apparently unrelated histories: penicillin and the changing expectations of medicine; the creation and neglect of national cancer institutions; nutritional anemia in Bombay; vitamin research; and the struggles of an immigrant chemist. All these paths converged on one experimental possibility—a chemical capable of interfering with leukemia-cell division.

17. The deeper meaning of Chapter 2

Medical victories can reveal new problems. As people lived longer and infectious diseases declined, cancer became more prominent. Science advances through unexpected connections. Minot and Wills were not studying cancer, but their discoveries about healthy blood laid the groundwork for chemotherapy. Failure can redirect a hypothesis, though it does not erase the harm done to patients in a failed experiment.

Finally, cancer research depended on politics and culture as much as on microscopes. The story of the NCI shows how funding can rise and disappear; Rosenow's experience shows how silence can isolate patients; Subbarao's career shows how opportunities are distributed unequally. Farber's central biological insight emerged in the middle of all these forces.

Chapter takeaway: If folate supplies materials needed for rapid cell division, a molecule that blocks folate's activity may suppress rapidly multiplying leukemia cells. Farber now has a plausible chemical strategy—but whether it can help a living child remains unanswered.
