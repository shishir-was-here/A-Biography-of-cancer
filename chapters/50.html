The final question: Can cancer ever end?

The last chapter returns to the question that has followed us from the Prologue:

Can human beings completely eliminate cancer?

Mukherjee recalls the atmosphere surrounding Sidney Farber's memorial in 1973. Farber's generation had dreamed of a sweeping victory over cancer, perhaps a universal cure achieved through medical science.

But decades of discoveries had revealed how complicated that dream was.

Cancer does not arise from a single foreign invader. It emerges when a cell's own genes and normal biological processes become altered.

To understand why total eradication may be difficult, we need to revisit the relationship between growth and life.

Why cancer is so deeply connected to our biology

Human bodies depend on cells that divide, repair damage, renew tissue, and respond to changes in their environment.

Those processes make life possible, but they also create opportunities for genetic errors.

Some mutations come from preventable exposures, such as carcinogens in cigarette smoke. Others can arise from errors during DNA copying or from internal processes that cannot be eliminated simply by avoiding one harmful substance.

Over time, certain mutations may disturb growth controls and contribute to malignancy.

This produces a difficult truth: the mechanisms that allow us to heal and renew ourselves are related to the mechanisms cancer can exploit.

Cancer is not unavoidable for every individual, and its risks can often be reduced. But preventing every cancer in every person is very different from preventing a particular exposure-related cancer.

A more useful definition of victory

Mukherjee asks whether the goal of cancer medicine should be total eradication or something more achievable and meaningful.

For example, if a society could prevent many people from dying of cancer prematurely, diagnose more tumors early, and transform some advanced cancers into manageable conditions, would that not be a profound achievement?

The book points toward an important idea associated with epidemiologist Richard Doll: death in old age may be inevitable, but much premature death is preventable.

This is not surrender. It is a more careful definition of success.

An absolute goal A practical and meaningful goal
No cancer ever occurs Fewer preventable cancers occur
Every cancer is permanently eliminated More cancers are cured or controlled
No patient ever dies Fewer people die prematurely or suffer unnecessarily
One universal cure Multiple strategies matched to different diseases

The author is not arguing against a cure. He is arguing that lives extended and suffering reduced are genuine victories even when mortality itself cannot be abolished.

Atossa returns from ancient Persia

To illustrate progress, Mukherjee introduces a thought experiment involving Atossa, the Persian queen whose breast illness was described by the historian Herodotus.

Imagine that Atossa lives through the centuries, with the same cancer appearing at the same stage each time she visits a different era.

This allows us to compare what physicians at different points in history might have offered her.

This is a teaching device, not a real medical record. We do not have the biological details of Atossa's actual illness, and the later genetic tests and treatment scenarios are hypothetical.

Ancient medicine: Recognition without effective treatment

In the imagined journey, Atossa first visits Imhotep's ancient Egyptian medical world. A breast tumor might be recognized, but no effective treatment is available.

In the Persian world of about 500 BC, Atossa seeks an operation on the affected breast. Later, Hippocrates gives the disease the name karkinos, a word associated with a crab.

Galen subsequently explains cancer using the mistaken theory of accumulated black bile. This idea will influence doctors for centuries.

Across much of the medieval period, treatment remains dangerous and poorly understood. Patients may encounter crude surgery, purging, topical substances, or remedies with no demonstrated ability to cure malignant disease.

The historical movement is not a straight line of constant progress. Bad explanations sometimes remain influential long after better observation becomes possible.

Surgery transforms cancer care—but reaches its limits

When Atossa arrives in the eighteenth century, John Hunter distinguishes between apparently local, removable tumors and advanced disease.

In the nineteenth century, anesthesia and antisepsis make more complex operations possible. William Halsted develops a radical mastectomy that removes the breast and additional nearby tissues.

This could control some cancers locally, but it often imposed severe physical costs. Later evidence demonstrated that increasingly extensive surgery did not automatically improve survival.

In the twentieth century, radiation provides another way of controlling tumors in a specific location. Over time, surgery and radiation become more measured and better selected.

Atossa's imagined treatment evolves from desperate, potentially mutilating operations toward treatment guided by evidence and the stage of disease.

Combination therapies bring new possibilities

Mukherjee then moves Atossa into the second half of the twentieth century.

She might receive adjuvant chemotherapy after surgery to reduce the risk that unseen cancer cells will cause a relapse.

If her hypothetical tumor is sensitive to estrogen, she might also receive tamoxifen, which interferes with estrogen-dependent growth signaling.

If the tumor is HER2-positive, targeted therapy with trastuzumab becomes another possibility.

These approaches attack cancer through different mechanisms:

Treatment Main purpose in the thought experiment
Surgery Remove local tumor tissue
Radiation Control disease in a defined area
Chemotherapy Attack malignant cells that may remain beyond the visible tumor
Hormonal therapy Reduce hormone-dependent growth signaling
HER2-targeted therapy Interfere with an abnormal molecular driver

The lesson is not that every patient should receive all these treatments. Choice depends on tumor stage, pathology, molecular features, benefits, and risks.

Genetic risk enters the family story

In the imagined 1990s, Atossa is also tested for an inherited harmful variant in BRCA1 or BRCA2 because of her young age at diagnosis and family considerations.

Mukherjee imagines that testing finds such a mutation. Her daughters are then offered risk assessment, more intensive screening, and preventive options.

The thought experiment shows how cancer medicine has moved beyond responding only when an obvious tumor is present. Researchers can now ask whether a person's inherited risk justifies earlier surveillance or prevention.

But an inherited mutation indicates risk, not an unavoidable destiny. Screening and preventive choices are highly individual and involve real tradeoffs.

Atossa in 2050: A future imagined in 2010

Mukherjee next moves Atossa into 2050, imagining that her doctor can read the complete genome of the tumor and use software to identify the pathways driving it.

She might receive a customized combination of targeted medicines. If the tumor develops resistance, the doctors could analyze the changing cancer and switch treatments.

This vision follows a progression developed across the book:

Observe tumor → identify cells → discover genes → map pathways → select treatment → monitor evolution.

The imagined 2050 scenario should be read as a forecast made by a writer in 2010, not as a promise or a verified description of medicine in 2050.

It captures the direction of scientific ambition rather than its guaranteed destination.

Why some cancers remain far harder than others

Atossa's breast-cancer example illustrates genuine progress, but Mukherjee warns against allowing one success story to represent all cancers.

A tumor confined to a treatable location and carrying a useful molecular target may respond well to modern treatment.

Other malignancies—for example, some advanced pancreatic and gallbladder cancers in the historical period discussed—may offer far fewer effective options.

Cancers differ in their genes, anatomy, speed of growth, timing of detection, and capacity to resist therapies.

There is no single biological disease called cancer with one uniform treatment response. This is why the search for one universal cure repeatedly proves misleading.

The past keeps returning in the future

Mukherjee reflects on the difficulty of predicting scientific breakthroughs. Penicillin and polio vaccines changed medicine in ways earlier generations could not foresee.

Yet cancer history also contains striking recurring themes.

The early observation that leukemia involved abnormal blood cells eventually led toward imatinib. Experiments involving viruses helped scientists discover oncogenes. Old observations about hormones and breast tissue helped lead toward endocrine therapies.

Research does not simply discard its past. New discoveries reinterpret old questions and bring them back into focus.

The tools change dramatically, but the scientific pattern often remains recognizable: observation, disagreement, experiment, evidence, revision, and renewed treatment attempts.

The word 'onkos' and the burden of cancer

The chapter returns to the Greek word onkos, an ancient term associated with a mass or burden and the linguistic root of oncology.

Mukherjee uses this word to reflect on cancer's weight: the physical weight of a growing tumor, the emotional weight carried by patients, and the historical burden of trying to understand and treat this disease.

But the image of a burden also suggests something carried forward. Each generation of physicians and patients inherits questions, observations, and unfinished work from the generations before it.

The biography of cancer is therefore also a biography of human persistence.

Germaine Berne: A second final story

The book ends with another patient, Germaine Berne, a psychologist from Alabama.

In 1999, she developed severe nausea and a persistent sense of fullness. Medical imaging identified a large mass near her stomach. A biopsy in January 2000 revealed a gastrointestinal stromal tumor (GIST).

Further examinations showed that the cancer had spread. The doctors believed surgery could not cure her, and at that time they had no reliably effective conventional chemotherapy for her advanced disease.

Germaine returned home expecting to die.

Her story could have ended there. Instead, it became closely tied to the very drug discovered through the CML research described earlier.

An unexpected second use for Gleevec

Germaine joined an online community of people living with GIST. Through these connections, she learned that Gleevec (imatinib) might work against her cancer as well.

The reason was molecular. Imatinib inhibits BCR-ABL in CML, but it can also inhibit certain other kinases, including KIT, a protein relevant to many GIST tumors.

This was an extraordinary example of how understanding a drug's molecular action could reveal another disease in which it might help.

Germaine gained access to a trial and started treatment in August 2001. Her tumors shrank, her energy returned, and her nausea improved dramatically.

She had been told to prepare for death. Now she regained years of life.

Resistance returns—and the options narrow

In 2004, Germaine's GIST developed resistance to imatinib. Tumors grew again in multiple locations.

She sought another experimental treatment, SU11248, through a trial in Boston. The new medicine produced a temporary improvement, but the disease soon progressed.

By February 2005, her symptoms had become overwhelming. She was in severe pain and could barely move normally.

This echoes the previous chapter's Red Queen metaphor. A treatment controls the disease; the disease adapts; a second treatment buys more time; eventually the options narrow.

The years between diagnosis and decline were not meaningless time. Germaine had rebuilt relationships, watched her daughter grow, and lived experiences that her original prognosis had seemed to rule out.

Germaine chooses what matters at the end

Mukherjee meets Germaine in the hospital near the end of her life.

By then, she does not want another trial or another drug. She wants to return home to Alabama and spend her remaining time according to her own priorities.

Her decision is not depicted as a failure of courage. It is an assertion of personal authority after years of submitting to scans, medicines, travel, side effects, and medical uncertainty.

Treating cancer is not the same as treating a person. A person may value relief from pain, time with family, freedom from invasive interventions, or the chance to die in familiar surroundings.

Good medical care must recognize the patient's goals, not only the tumor's measurements.

Why this is the book's final image

At the beginning of the book, Carla entered the hospital in fear, and Mukherjee struggled to explain the diagnosis. At the end, Germaine confronts the limits of treatment with a clarity born from years of struggle.

Both women embody the central truth of the biography: cancer is a biological process, but its history can never be separated from the people who live with it.

The author has followed four thousand years of observation and intervention—ancient surgeons, cellular pathologists, radiologists, chemists, politicians, activists, geneticists, and patients.

The final image is not a microscope or a perfect cure. It is a person trying to preserve dignity and control within the limits imposed by illness.

What the whole book leaves us with

Cancer is extraordinarily difficult to eliminate because it arises from the very cellular processes that sustain life. Nevertheless, prevention, surgery, radiation, chemotherapy, hormonal treatment, targeted drugs, and molecular diagnostics have changed what many diagnoses mean.

The future of cancer medicine may involve ever more personalized treatments and ongoing adaptation. But scientific progress must always be evaluated by what happens to real patients—not only by discoveries in laboratories.

Final takeaway: The history of cancer is not a simple story of inevitable victory or inevitable defeat. It is a story of people continually learning how to prevent suffering, prolong meaningful life, and act with humanity even when a cure is beyond reach.
