## 1. A larger battle begins: Farber needs more than a laboratory

At the end of Part One, Farber had shown that a chemical could make childhood leukemia temporarily retreat. It was a historic possibility, but experiments in a single hospital could not defeat a disease that killed people throughout the country. To move further, he needed additional drugs, larger studies, trained physicians, laboratories, and sustained support.

Mukherjee opens the chapter with the nineteenth-century French observer **Alexis de Tocqueville**, who had noticed Americans' extraordinary willingness to form associations. When they wanted to change society, they organized clubs, charities, committees, and societies. Farber recognized the same possibility for cancer medicine: rather than depending only on his own laboratory, he could build a movement.

The Jimmy Fund had demonstrated that ordinary people could be persuaded to help children with cancer. But the scale Farber imagined required federal money. Congress controlled resources far larger than anything his charity could raise. He needed someone who knew how to turn public sympathy into national policy. **That person was Mary Lasker.**

## 2. Mary Lasker's unusual preparation for medical activism

**Mary Woodard Lasker** was born in Watertown, Wisconsin, in 1900. Her mother, Sara Johnson, had worked her way up through a Chicago department store and later applied her sales talent to public projects. Mary learned that a persuasive idea could be presented, promoted, and sold just as effectively as a commercial product.

After graduating from Radcliffe, Mary sold European paintings in New York. Later she established **Hollywood Patterns**, a successful business selling ready-to-use dress designs. These experiences developed skills that medical researchers seldom possessed: networking, negotiating, raising money, recognizing an audience, and making complex ideas sound urgent and understandable.

In 1939 she met **Albert Lasker**, a successful advertising executive at Lord and Thomas. They married after a rapid courtship. Albert believed effective advertising should explain clearly *why* a person should act. To him, an advertisement was not merely a striking image; it was an argument organized for maximum impact.

When Mary began a public campaign against disease, she would use those very principles. **She knew how to make people pay attention before she knew the details of cancer biology.**

## 3. Why illness became personal for Mary

Mukherjee gives us three vivid memories from Mary's early life. As a child she survived an illness so severe that someone doubted she would live. On a visit to a family laundress, she saw a woman recovering from the surgical removal of both breasts for cancer, surrounded by the difficulties of poverty and raising children. And during the devastating influenza epidemic of 1918, she experienced the terror of illness directly.

These experiences left her with two impressions: disease could destroy ordinary life without warning, but medicine occasionally had the power to interrupt that destruction. She wanted the second possibility to become far more common.

A later family tragedy intensified her determination. In 1939 her mother suffered a heart attack followed by a disabling stroke. Mary searched for treatments and was astonished by how little medicine could offer. Her mother died in 1940. Mary began to see the fight against heart disease and cancer as a moral mission rather than one philanthropic option among many.

**Important idea:** For Mary, the purpose of research funding was not to produce elegant papers. It was to spare other families experiences like her own.

## 4. A professional skill is redirected: advertising becomes advocacy

Albert Lasker became Mary's partner in her new mission. He understood how institutions attracted donations and how public messages could change attitudes. Their social world included wealthy donors, executives, elected officials, journalists, and influential families. Mary understood that each of these connections could become a supporter of medical research.

The couple treated fundraising almost as a profession. They did more than ask people for money; they developed continuing relationships with people who could influence others. Mukherjee presents this as a distinctive form of power: **friend-raising could eventually become fund-raising, and fund-raising could become legislation.**

This is an important turn in the book's story. Farber's earlier work was driven by measurements, experiments, and patients. Now those results would meet the very different machinery of publicity, lobbying, political alliances, and budgets. Neither machinery was sufficient by itself.

## 5. The American Society for the Control of Cancer

Mary investigated an existing organization: the **American Society for the Control of Cancer (ASCC)**. She discovered a society with scientific and educational aspirations but limited influence and money. The group was not organized to become the powerful public pressure campaign she envisioned.

In 1943 she helped persuade *Reader's Digest* to publish articles about cancer detection. The response was startling. Readers sent letters, personal stories, small gifts, and donations; some correspondents had lost relatives to cancer. Even a soldier serving in the Pacific sent money in memory of his mother. Contributions connected a distant national issue with private family grief.

The magazine received about **$300,000** in contributions over the following months—more than the ASCC's annual budget. That result confirmed Mary's intuition: Americans cared intensely about cancer when given a clear opportunity to respond.

## 6. From a quiet society to the American Cancer Society

Mary and Albert recruited advertising executive **Emerson Foote** and other business and public-relations figures to reorganize the ASCC. The new leadership believed that doctors and researchers had been too focused on professional details and too timid about communicating with the general public.

The organization was renamed the **American Cancer Society (ACS)**. The campaign dramatically increased printed educational material, posters, donation boxes, fundraising activities, and public appeals. The amounts raised surged: the book reports approximately **$832,000 in 1944**, **$4.3 million in 1945**, and **$12 million in 1947**.

This dramatic transformation was not peaceful. **Clarence Little**, the society's president, felt displaced by outsiders who valued publicity and fundraising over expert control. By 1945, the conflict ended with Little's resignation. The new lay leadership had taken over.

| Older approach | Laskerite approach |
|---|---|
| Dominated by medical professionals | Major roles for organizers, businesspeople, and advocates |
| Technical memoranda and limited outreach | Public campaigns, fundraising, and direct political pressure |
| Relatively modest resources | Rapidly expanding donor base |
| Emphasis on educating medical specialists | Emphasis on mobilizing ordinary citizens and Congress |

Neither approach was free of risk. A public movement could raise unprecedented support, but a movement led largely by non-scientists might push promises faster than evidence could justify.

## 7. The Laskerites set their sights on Congress

The new society had learned how to raise donations, but Mary was thinking beyond private charity. Federal support could fund far larger research programs over many years. The movement's next aim was therefore to change government spending on cancer.

Her allies became known as the **Laskerites**. They were united by urgency, public persuasion, and a conviction that the country was neglecting a treatable scientific problem. Yet Mary knew that a campaign made entirely of advertisers, lobbyists, and wealthy patrons would lack medical legitimacy.

She needed someone who could explain chemotherapy credibly to senators and persuade scientists that the campaign deserved support. She needed a doctor who could move comfortably between a laboratory and a hearing room.

## 8. The alliance with Sidney Farber

Farber and Lasker began corresponding after his early antifolate results drew attention. An introduction by National Cancer Institute director **John Heller** helped bring Farber's work to her notice. The suggestion of a drug that might act like a cancer-fighting equivalent of penicillin fascinated her.

Farber wrote detailed letters explaining his research. Mary replied with political ideas, questions, and opportunities. Soon they were discussing something far larger than an individual drug: an organized, federally supported attack on cancer itself.

Their strengths complemented one another:

- **Farber** supplied scientific credibility, experience with patients, and an insistence that medicine could act before every question had been answered.
- **Lasker** supplied fundraising talent, a national network, political strategy, and the ability to make research sound urgent to the public.

Their partnership was more than convenient. Each had been pursuing a mission the other could make possible. Farber needed political power, and Lasker needed a scientific case.

## 9. What the chapter title means

**“They form a society”** refers both to Tocqueville's observation of American civic life and to the campaign's transformation into a national organization. Cancer research would no longer depend exclusively on isolated doctors pursuing individual discoveries.

It would increasingly be shaped by an organized public demanding that the government act. This was the beginning of the modern cancer lobby.

The title is hopeful, but it also contains a warning. **Organizing a movement is not the same thing as understanding a disease.** Political force can produce laboratories and budgets; it cannot by itself produce a cure.

## 11. The deeper meaning: science needs society, but society needs science

One lesson is that a major medical problem is not solved by intellectual brilliance alone. Researchers need funds, institutions, staff, equipment, and public trust. Another is that private experiences can become the motivation for large public changes: Mary's family losses helped fuel national activism.

At the same time, the chapter introduces a tension that will define Part Two. Fundraisers naturally want a clear message, an inspiring goal, and visible progress. Cancer science is slow, uncertain, and resistant to simple promises. The alliance of Farber and Lasker will accomplish extraordinary things, but those different cultures will not always agree.

**Chapter takeaway:** Farber and Lasker join two kinds of power—scientific experiment and public organization. Their partnership turns the fight against cancer from a collection of local studies into a campaign that aims to reshape American medical research.
