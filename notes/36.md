## 1. A treatment becomes popular before its benefit is proved

At the beginning of this chapter, **high-dose chemotherapy with marrow or stem-cell rescue** is becoming increasingly common for breast cancer. Hospitals invest in transplant units, specialists offer the procedure, and patients hear that an aggressive new approach may succeed when conventional treatment fails.

Yet the central scientific question remains unresolved: **does this treatment help women live longer than standard therapy?** The fact that a procedure is available at a major hospital does not answer that question.

Mukherjee depicts an unsettling situation in which medical practice expands faster than the controlled evidence needed to justify it.

## 2. Why everyone wanted the most aggressive option

Patients with advanced breast cancer faced terrifying choices. Many had already endured surgery, radiation, or ordinary chemotherapy, only to see the disease return. A new treatment that seemed more powerful offered the possibility of overcoming resistance.

Doctors, too, wanted a breakthrough. Hospitals invested money and professional prestige in transplant programs. The price of treatment could range from tens to hundreds of thousands of dollars.

Different pressures reinforced one another: desperate hope, commercial incentives, dramatic individual responses, and the belief that more intensive treatment must mean more effective treatment.

## 3. Nelene Fox and the insurance dispute (1991)

The chapter follows **Nelene Fox**, a thirty-eight-year-old California schoolteacher and mother of three. Diagnosed with breast cancer, she later suffered metastatic relapse after other treatments.

Her physicians suggested an **autologous bone marrow transplant** as a possible last resort. Fox wanted the procedure, but her health insurer, **Health Net**, refused to pay because it considered the treatment **investigational**, meaning its clinical benefit had not been established.

From the insurer's perspective, the procedure was unproven. From Fox's perspective, an organization appeared to be denying her a chance at survival.

## 4. A community mobilizes to help her

Fox began a public fundraising campaign. Friends, relatives, neighbors, and strangers contributed through local events, donations, and community efforts. Supporters rallied against the insurer, and her brother **Mark Hiepler**, an attorney, challenged the denial.

By the summer of **1992**, Fox had raised approximately **$220,000** from thousands of contributors—enough to undergo the procedure without insurance approval.

The fundraising was an extraordinary demonstration of public solidarity. But it also revealed a dangerous gap between the emotional conviction that a treatment was needed and the scientific uncertainty about whether it would work.

## 5. The transplant and its outcome

Fox underwent high-dose chemotherapy and marrow transplantation in **August 1992**. The regimen was difficult and carried serious complications.

Her cancer later relapsed widely, including in major organs and the brain. She died in **April 1993**, aged forty, leaving her husband and three daughters.

Her death was heartbreaking. But it could not by itself tell researchers whether the therapy was useful across a population. A single tragic outcome might occur even with an effective treatment; likewise, a single dramatic response might occur with an ineffective one.

Reliable evaluation required fair comparisons involving many patients.

## 6. Why the lawsuit had a larger effect

Fox's family pursued legal action against Health Net. Her brother argued that the procedure could hardly be treated as experimental when so many hospitals were already offering it.

In **December 1993**, a jury awarded her family **$89 million** in damages; the case was later resolved out of court for a smaller undisclosed amount.

The judgment sent a forceful signal to insurers. Many became more willing to cover high-dose breast-cancer treatment. But court decisions about access did not settle the underlying medical question. A treatment can be widespread, expensive, and passionately defended while still lacking proof of benefit.

## 7. The access-versus-evidence dilemma

Advocacy campaigns—especially those influenced by the AIDS crisis—had shown that desperately ill patients needed a voice in decisions about experimental medicine. This was a crucial change in the history of patient rights.

But access to unproven treatments created a second problem. If nearly everyone could obtain a procedure outside a clinical trial, fewer people might agree to participate in randomized comparisons.

Why accept a chance of being assigned standard therapy when everyone around you claims the new treatment is the only hope? The treatment could therefore become harder to evaluate precisely because it had become so popular.

## 8. Werner Bezwoda offers spectacular results

A South African oncologist, **Werner Bezwoda**, reported exceptionally strong responses to high-dose chemotherapy at the University of Witwatersrand in Johannesburg. His claims attracted attention around the world.

At a **1992** cancer meeting, he presented figures suggesting complete responses in more than ninety percent of treated women—far better than results reported by many other institutions.

These claims made Bezwoda a powerful symbol for advocates of transplantation. If his results were correct, perhaps the method truly could achieve something extraordinary.

## 9. Thousands receive a still-unproven procedure

As the 1990s progressed, high-dose chemotherapy and transplantation became an enormous medical enterprise. Hospitals built specialized facilities, and thousands of women received treatment.

The book estimates that roughly **40,000 women worldwide** underwent marrow transplantation for breast cancer between **1991 and 1999**, at a total cost of billions of dollars.

These historical figures illustrate the scale of the phenomenon. The unsettling question is how such widespread use could precede convincing evidence that the treatment improved survival.

## 10. Clinical trial results begin to conflict (1999)

At a major oncology meeting in **Atlanta in 1999**, several studies of high-dose chemotherapy were presented. The results did not tell a consistent story.

Some trials showed no clear survival benefit. Others reported serious toxicity. In one study discussed by the author, dozens of women receiving transplants died from treatment-related complications.

Bezwoda's study, by contrast, appeared dramatically positive. His data seemed to suggest a large survival advantage—making his results the main argument keeping the treatment movement alive.

How could one research group obtain spectacular benefits when several others found little or none?

## 11. The Johannesburg investigation

In **December 1999**, investigators requested permission to inspect Bezwoda's patient records. He agreed, and reviewers traveled to Johannesburg.

They encountered serious irregularities. Many records were missing or inadequate. There were major inconsistencies in patient eligibility, reported outcomes, and the supposed comparison groups. Consent and institutional-review documentation could not be established for important parts of the work.

Some records did not match the trial as described. The investigators became convinced that the reported study could not be trusted.

## 12. A scientific fraud is exposed (2000)

In **February 2000**, Bezwoda admitted that he had falsified aspects of his study. He resigned from his university position.

The apparent miracle had depended on unreliable and fabricated research. The consequences were far-reaching because Bezwoda's claims had influenced doctors, patients, insurers, and public debate about an expensive, dangerous treatment.

Scientific fraud harms more than the reputation of the dishonest researcher. It can expose patients to needless risk and make it harder for legitimate trials to answer urgent questions.

## 13. Randomized trials give the decisive answer

Later controlled studies failed to demonstrate the hoped-for survival benefit of very-high-dose chemotherapy with marrow rescue for the breast-cancer settings examined in the book.

The author describes a large trial in which some women suffered fatal treatment complications, while others developed serious treatment-related **acute myeloid leukemia**. These harms were especially difficult to justify in the absence of improved cancer outcomes.

The result undercut the assumption that an apparently more powerful treatment must be better. The clinical evidence had finally caught up with—and overturned—the enthusiasm.

## 14. Maggie Keswick Jencks and the search for a map

Mukherjee introduces **Maggie Keswick Jencks**, a Scottish landscape artist who developed breast cancer, underwent treatment, and later suffered metastatic recurrence. She also received high-dose chemotherapy and an autologous transplant.

Her remission did not last. She died in **July 1995**. In her writing, she compared the experience of a cancer diagnosis to being suddenly thrown out of an airplane with a parachute into an unfamiliar landscape.

Doctors had provided equipment for an emergency landing, but little guidance about the terrain below: where the danger was, what would happen next, and how to find one's way.

## 15. What “the map and the parachute” means

The **parachute** represents urgent intervention: medicines, surgery, transplantation, and all the technical tools doctors can offer. The **map** represents understanding: knowledge of cancer biology, likely behavior, treatment benefit, uncertainty, and the patient's own possible paths.

The metaphor criticizes a style of medicine obsessed with inventing stronger interventions without adequately understanding the disease or helping patients make sense of their situation.

Patients need both effective tools and intelligible guidance. One without the other can leave them frightened, disoriented, or exposed to harmful decisions.

## 16. John Bailar asks whether the war was being won

In **1997**, epidemiologist **John Bailar**, working with **Heather Gornik**, published a broad assessment of cancer mortality. The title suggested that cancer remained undefeated.

They examined age-adjusted cancer death rates over time. According to the book, overall mortality had increased slightly between **1970 and 1994**, from about **189 to 201 deaths per 100,000**, despite major investments in cancer research.

At first glance, these numbers might suggest failure. But Mukherjee asks readers to examine what lies beneath the total.

## 17. One total can conceal opposite trends

Some cancers had become less deadly. Mortality from cervical and certain colorectal cancers fell; several childhood cancers, Hodgkin lymphoma, and testicular cancer benefited from better treatment or earlier detection.

But lung cancer exerted an enormous countervailing effect, particularly among older women after decades of rising cigarette use. The book describes a dramatic increase in lung-cancer mortality among women above fifty-five during the period examined.

Thus a nearly flat or slightly worsening national total could hide **real progress in some cancers combined with deterioration in others**. The overall number was a dynamic balance, not proof that nothing had changed.

## 18. Why age adjustment matters

Cancer risk tends to rise with age. If a country's population grows older, the raw number of cancer deaths can increase even when the underlying risk at each age remains stable or improves.

**Age-adjustment** uses a common reference age distribution so that death rates from different years can be compared more meaningfully.

For example, comparing a young population from one decade with an older population from another could make cancer appear to have become much more dangerous simply because there are more older people. Age adjustment helps separate changes in population structure from changes in disease risk and outcomes.

## 19. The shift away from universal solutions

Mukherjee closes the chapter with the realization that cancer is not a single disease waiting for one perfect weapon. Different cancers arise in different tissues, depend on different biological changes, and respond differently to treatment or prevention.

**Richard Klausner**, then director of the National Cancer Institute, argued against the expectation that one universal treatment—or one universal prevention strategy—could conquer every cancer.

This prepares the transition into Part Five: if more aggressive treatment is not a universal answer, scientists need better **maps of cancer's underlying mechanisms**.

## 20. Important terms and final takeaway

| Term | Simple meaning |
|---|---|
| Investigational treatment | A treatment whose effectiveness or safety is still being studied |
| Autologous transplantation | Returning a patient's own collected blood-forming cells after treatment |
| Randomized trial | A clinical comparison in which treatment groups are assigned systematically, usually by chance |
| Treatment-related mortality | Death caused by complications of treatment rather than directly by the original cancer |
| Age-adjusted mortality | Death rate standardized to reduce distortion from differences in population age |
| Scientific fraud | Deliberately falsifying or fabricating research evidence |

The chapter's most important lesson is twofold. **Hope and availability do not prove that a cancer treatment works**, and **overall cancer statistics can hide very different trends among diseases and populations**.

Cancer medicine needed a new kind of progress: less blind escalation of treatment and a much deeper understanding of the cells and genes that make each cancer behave as it does.
