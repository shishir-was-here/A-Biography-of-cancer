## 1. A blackboard filled with possible cures

In 1963, researcher **George Canellos** enters the NCI and finds **Tom Frei** at a blackboard connecting drug names with cancer types. On one side are agents such as vincristine, methotrexate, and other cytotoxic medicines. On the other side are diseases such as lymphoma, breast cancer, and lung cancer.

The diagram captures the excitement of the time. If combinations could make leukemia disappear, perhaps researchers could discover useful combinations for many other tumors.

But most of the drugs were **cytotoxic**—they damaged or killed dividing cells. They were not precisely designed to recognize a unique abnormality of a specific cancer. Their usefulness depended on whether cancer cells were more vulnerable than the patient's normal tissues.

The new question was not merely, “Can chemicals kill cancer cells?” It was: **Can intensive combinations produce a lasting cure in a second, different form of cancer?**

## 2. Why Hodgkin lymphoma was the logical next target

As Chapter 20 explained, **Henry Kaplan** had shown that carefully selected localized Hodgkin lymphoma could respond well to extended-field radiation.

But advanced Hodgkin disease was different. The cancer had spread beyond one region, and local radiation could not easily eradicate every site. Physicians needed a treatment that traveled through the body.

Hodgkin lymphoma therefore offered a bridge between leukemia and the more varied world of solid cancers. If combination chemotherapy could cure widespread Hodgkin disease, the new approach would seem much more broadly promising.

Researchers including **Gordon Zubrod**, **Vincent DeVita**, and **George Canellos** set out to test this possibility.

## 3. Vincent DeVita and the early MOMP regimen

DeVita joined the NCI in 1963. He was bold, competitive, and attracted to the experimental spirit of Frei and Freireich. He believed critics needed proof that drugs could cure an advanced malignancy.

The first four-drug regimen used for Hodgkin lymphoma was **MOMP**, combining methotrexate, vincristine (Oncovin), nitrogen mustard, and prednisone. Its early results suggested the disease could respond, but the treatment was so toxic that patients required close medical supervision and often hospitalization.

Other doctors were alarmed. The regimen exposed patients with serious cancer to multiple poisons whose combined effects were not fully understood. Frei helped protect the experiment from being shut down immediately.

## 4. MOPP: A stronger combination

In **1964**, DeVita replaced methotrexate with **procarbazine** and extended treatment. This created a new regimen, **MOPP**:

| Letter | Drug |
|---|---|
| M | Mechlorethamine, also called nitrogen mustard |
| O | Oncovin, another name for vincristine |
| P | Procarbazine |
| P | Prednisone |

DeVita and colleagues treated patients with advanced Hodgkin lymphoma, many of whom had already failed radiation or other drugs. In the study described in the book, **43 patients** were treated.

The side effects were severe, especially nausea. Some patients traveled long distances to receive treatment and suffered intensely on the way home. Others experienced infections or profound reductions in blood counts.

## 5. What made the MOPP results remarkable?

After months of treatment, swollen lymph nodes decreased or disappeared in many patients. Organs compressed by lymphoma began functioning better. Some patients regained strength and weight.

The book reports that **35 of the 43 patients reached complete remission**, and more than half of the initial group were ultimately cured over longer follow-up.

This was extraordinary for advanced Hodgkin lymphoma, which had previously been overwhelmingly fatal. It was not merely a temporary dip in a blood test; a substantial group of people survived without their cancer returning.

Yet the results should not be generalized to every cancer. The success belonged to a specific disease, a specific historical regimen, and a carefully studied group of patients.

## 6. The hidden costs of cure

MOPP revealed the double-edged nature of cytotoxic therapy. It could cure lymphoma, but it could also damage normal cells profoundly.

The book describes several severe complications: nausea, infections during immune suppression, permanent infertility in some patients, and, years later, **second cancers**, including forms of leukemia caused by prior chemotherapy.

This creates a hard problem for oncology. A treatment that saves a patient from one cancer may produce lasting health problems—or, rarely, another cancer in the future.

**Cure is not always a return to the state of health that existed before the illness.** Survivorship can include permanent medical consequences that must be weighed against the danger of the original cancer.

## 7. Childhood leukemia still needed a better solution

Meanwhile, the early VAMP experiments had shown that leukemia could be driven into deep remission. But nervous-system relapses and other failures kept cure rates low.

One researcher, **Donald Pinkel**, believed the answer might be to treat leukemia more thoroughly. He had trained under Farber and later moved to Memphis, Tennessee, to build a new childhood leukemia program at **St. Jude Children's Research Hospital**.

When Pinkel arrived in **1961**, the institution had limited resources and little established research infrastructure. He built a team willing to investigate difficult regimens for a disease many physicians still considered nearly hopeless.

## 8. Pinkel's idea: “Total Therapy”

Pinkel and his colleagues took several earlier discoveries and combined them into a coordinated strategy. Their approach had four major elements:

1. **Multiple agents:** Use different drugs across the treatment course to attack cancer through more than one mechanism.
2. **Central nervous system treatment:** Deliver some chemotherapy directly into spinal fluid to reach places ordinary intravenous drugs could not.
3. **Brain-directed radiation in the historical protocol:** Treat potential nervous-system disease that might be hidden behind the blood–brain barrier.
4. **Long treatment duration:** Continue treatment well beyond the first remission—sometimes for two or more years—to reduce the risk of relapse.

Pinkel called the strategy **Total Therapy**. It was an ambitious attempt to eliminate both visible disease and the hidden cells that could later restart leukemia.

Modern leukemia treatment has evolved significantly, including how doctors prevent nervous-system relapse, but the historical principle of treating beyond initial remission remains important.

## 9. Why Total Therapy was so difficult for patients

The treatment involved repeated blood tests, spinal taps, marrow examinations, intravenous medicines, transfusions, and hospital visits. Some patients endured years of treatment. Families had to reorganize ordinary life around the demands of hospitals and recovery.

The doctors were trying to save children, but their methods were punishing. The treatment was so demanding that even some senior researchers were reluctant to direct its trials.

Mukherjee connects this historical story with **Carla Reed**, his adult leukemia patient. Carla experiences cycles of chemotherapy and radiation, fatigue, hair loss, loneliness, and the gradual erosion of her desire to engage with other people.

Her medical schedule is filled with appointments, tests, and treatments. Friends and even medical staff may want emotional responses from her, but she has little energy left for anyone else's needs.

This is a vital insight: **patients do not exist to reassure the people caring for them.** They are the ones carrying the disease and its treatment.

## 10. The first striking results from St. Jude

In **1968**, Pinkel's team reported preliminary results from a small study of **31 patients**. **27** had reached remission. The interval before relapse had lengthened dramatically compared with Farber's earliest patients.

Even more important, **13 children had not relapsed** and were alive after completing treatment at the time of the report. Some had been in remission for years.

These numbers had to be interpreted carefully: the trial was small and not randomized. Yet it offered far stronger hope of durable disease control than physicians had previously seen.

## 11. Longer follow-up changes the meaning of leukemia

Pinkel's team continued developing and studying Total Therapy through multiple protocols. In a 1979 review of eight trials, the book reports that **278 patients** had finished their medicine and stopped chemotherapy. About **80% remained disease-free** at the point of follow-up described by the author.

This did not mean every child with leukemia everywhere had an 80% cure rate. It meant the St. Jude protocols had produced a remarkable long-term result in the patients analyzed.

The central transformation was clear. Childhood acute lymphoblastic leukemia could no longer be treated as invariably fatal. Physicians had evidence that many children could survive after intensive, carefully structured therapy.

## 13. The deeper meaning: A victory with a price

This chapter presents genuine achievements. Unlike earlier decades, in which physicians could mostly watch aggressive leukemia and lymphoma progress, doctors now had treatments capable of producing lasting cures in some patients.

But the chapter refuses to erase the suffering that made those cures possible. Chemotherapy caused terrible physical effects and created new medical risks. Carla's experience makes the point that even an effective regimen can overwhelm an individual's daily life.

The broader lesson is also scientific: success with leukemia and Hodgkin lymphoma does not prove a universal cure exists. Different cancers have different biology, and therapies that work in one may fail completely in another.

**Chapter takeaway:** Combination chemotherapy proves capable of curing some advanced Hodgkin lymphoma and transforming childhood leukemia survival. The achievements are historic, but they emerge from demanding treatments that create new ethical and medical challenges.
